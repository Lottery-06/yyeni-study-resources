## What the curve shows

### The boundary of the possible

A production possibility curve (PPC) is a boundary showing the maximum combinations of two goods an economy can produce in a given period when its resources are employed fully and efficiently. Picture an economy that makes canned fish at Walvis Bay and maize meal at Otavi. Send every worker and every boat to fish and it lands one end of the boundary, say 30 000 tonnes of fish and no maize meal; send every worker to the mill and it lands the other end, 60 000 tonnes of maize meal and no fish. The PPC joins the maximum combinations between those two ends.

### The assumptions beneath the drawing

The curve is drawn for a given state of resources and technology: the stock of workers, boats, looms and land, and the know-how worked into them, are held unchanged for the period the curve covers. Two goods are shown because a flat drawing cannot carry more than two axes; real economies make thousands of goods, so a PPC for Japan groups everything into two bundles - vehicles at one axis and all other output at the other - and the boundary between them shows the same trade-off.

### Why it slopes down and bows out

The boundary slopes downward from left to right because resources are scarce: producing one more tonne of maize meal means pulling workers and tins off the fish line, so fish output falls, and that sacrifice is the opportunity cost of the maize. The curve tends to bow outward because resources are not equally suited to both uses: the first cooks moved from the cannery to the mill cost little fish, because they were poor at canning; the last ones cost a great deal. The trade-off grows as a move along the boundary continues.

## Under, on and beyond

### Three locations, three meanings

A point on the boundary shows resources fully and efficiently employed: output sits at a maximum for that combination. A point under the boundary, inside it, shows resources idle or used in the wrong place: output of one good, or of both, could be higher without a sacrifice. A point beyond the boundary cannot be produced with the current resources and technology; it stays out of reach until the boundary itself shifts outward. Location is a fact, not a verdict: an economy inside its PPC during a seasonal pause is not failing, it is waiting for the harvest.

### A worked reading

A co-operative in Katima Mulilo weaves reed mats and carves fish baskets. With its 12 members it could make up to 60 reed mats or 30 fish baskets a month; 40 reed mats together with 22 fish baskets is one combination on its boundary. In March it made 40 reed mats and 12 fish baskets, with four members away and a loom idle. At the same 40 reed mats, 12 fish baskets sits below the 22 the boundary allows, so March lies inside the boundary: both outputs could have been higher had the same people and loom been at work.

### From idle to employed

When resources are idle, moving toward the boundary costs nothing in the other good: the four members returning raise mats and baskets together, since the co-operative starts inside its PPC. That is why unemployment of any resource shows up as an interior point. Once the boundary is reached, further gains in one good come at a cost in the other, and the interior free lunch ends. The Netherlands pumps land from the sea to move its whole boundary outward, and a point beyond reach today becomes reachable once the boundary has moved.

## Movements along the boundary

### Sliding, not moving the boundary

A movement along a PPC is a reallocation of resources between the two goods while the economy stays on the boundary: more of one good, less of the other, with the maximum possible output unchanged. Sliding toward the fish axis raises fish and lowers maize; the curve itself has not moved. Portugal shifting workers and presses from cork plantations into machine works slides along its PPC: the capacity to do either stays as it was, and the choice changes.

### The cost of the slide, in figures

An economy boundary runs from 60 tonnes of maize to 30 tonnes of fish. From A, 45 tonnes of maize and 15 tonnes of fish, it slides to B, 25 tonnes of maize and 25 tonnes of fish: 25 - 15 = 10 tonnes of fish gained and 45 - 25 = 20 tonnes of maize given up, 2 tonnes of maize for each tonne of fish. A further slide to C, 10 tonnes of maize and 28 tonnes of fish, gains 28 - 25 = 3 tonnes of fish for a sacrifice of 25 - 10 = 15 tonnes of maize, 5 tonnes of maize for each tonne of fish. The cost per tonne rises as the slide continues, because the resources least suited to fishing were moved first.

### Movement or shift?

Two ideas are often run together: sliding along the boundary, and moving the boundary itself. In a movement along the PPC the economy keeps the same curve and swaps one good for the other; in a shift the whole curve relocates and the maximum possible output changes. The test is to ask what happened to the economy capacity rather than its choice: an unchanged maximum means a movement along the curve; a maximum that has risen or fallen means the curve itself shifted.

## Shifts of the boundary

### The boundary itself moves

A shift of the PPC moves the whole boundary rather than a point sliding along it. An outward shift shows the economy productive capacity has grown: more of at least one good can be produced than before. An inward shift shows capacity has shrunk. The shift is about what the economy could do, not what it chose: a country that builds workshops and trains technicians has shifted its boundary outward even if its actual output point stays where it was.

### A shift, in figures

A weaving co-operative in Gobabis trains its 15 members on faster looms. Before the training its boundary ran from 90 scarves to 45 blankets a week; after it, the same members and looms can reach up to 120 scarves or 60 blankets a week. The whole boundary has moved outward: 120 - 90 = 30 more scarves and 60 - 45 = 15 more blankets. A combination beyond the old boundary, such as 100 scarves a week, now sits inside the new one. Growth of capacity is shown by the new curve, not by a slide along the old one.

### What moves the boundary

Capacity grows when an economy gains resources or gets more out of them: more workers, more machines, better training, or technology that raises what an hour of work produces. Vietnam two decades of building factories and technical schools have pushed its boundary outward, which is why output once beyond reach is now routine there. Capacity shrinks when resources are destroyed: a flood that washes away looms and yarn shifts the co-operative boundary inward, and the maximum of both goods falls together. The consequences land on consumers, who face whatever the new boundary permits, and on workers, whose jobs depend on the capacity it describes.
