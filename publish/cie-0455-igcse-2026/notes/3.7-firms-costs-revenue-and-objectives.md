## What production costs are

### The payments the firm itself makes

The costs of production are the payments a firm makes for the inputs it uses in producing its output, and its total cost is the sum of its fixed costs and its variable costs. A bakery in Otjiwarongo pays rent for its shop, wages to its bakers, and the flour, yeast and electricity its ovens consume; added up over a month, these payments are the firm's costs of production. A fish cannery at Walvis Bay makes the same kinds of payment for premises, people and materials. What matters at this level is that each cost is a payment the firm itself makes.

### Fixed and variable costs

Costs divide by how they respond to output. A fixed cost does not change with the level of output: rent, insurance and the lease on a printing press are paid at the same figure whether the firm makes one unit or one thousand. A variable cost changes in step with output: the vinyl and ink that Namib Signs burns, the fruit that Kaoko Juice bottles and the hours its casual pickers work rise and fall with the units made. A motor-parts maker in Germany faces the same split - its plant lease in Stuttgart is fixed, while its steel deliveries move with production.

### The firm's costs and costs imposed on others

Two ideas are often run together here: the firm's own costs of production, and costs imposed on people outside the firm. The firm's production costs are the payments it makes itself. External costs are the costs its activity imposes on others with no payment - a dairy whose wastewater fouls a river imposes a cost it does not pay. Social costs are the whole cost to society: the firm's own production costs together with the costs imposed on others. The test is who makes the payment.

## Working out the firm's costs

### The working formulas

Total cost = fixed cost + variable cost. Average total cost = total cost / output; average fixed cost = fixed cost / output; average variable cost = variable cost / output. Otjiwa Dairy in Gobabis pays N$6 000 a month in fixed costs and N$4 for the ingredients of each litre it processes. At 3 000 litres a month its variable costs come to N$4 x 3 000 = N$12 000, so total cost = N$6 000 + N$12 000 = N$18 000.

### The averages, worked through

At those 3 000 litres, average fixed cost = N$6 000 / 3 000 litres = N$2.00 per litre, average variable cost = N$12 000 / 3 000 = N$4.00 per litre, and average total cost = N$18 000 / 3 000 = N$6.00 per litre. At 6 000 litres, variable costs reach N$4 x 6 000 = N$24 000, so total cost = N$6 000 + N$24 000 = N$30 000 and average total cost = N$30 000 / 6 000 = N$5.00 per litre: average fixed cost has fallen to N$1.00 while average variable cost held at N$4.00. A juice bottler in Vietnam runs the same arithmetic on its own figures.

### Why output moves the averages

Average fixed cost falls as output rises because the same fixed payment is spread over more units, and this tends to pull average total cost down at first. Average variable cost is steadier: where each extra litre costs the dairy N$4 in ingredients, the average holds at N$4.00. Where output strains capacity - overtime pay, machine breakdowns, a crowded cold room - the cost of one more unit rises, and average variable cost and average total cost turn upward with it.

## What the firm takes in

### Income from selling

Revenue is the income a firm receives from selling its output over a period, measured in money and before any costs are deducted. It is read in two ways: total revenue, the whole income from sales in the period, and average revenue, that income per unit sold. A lodge at the Etosha gate that fills 20 rooms at N$1 500 a night takes in N$1 500 x 20 = N$30 000 for those nights; that income is its revenue, whether or not it covers the lodge's costs. A bus operator in Windhoek, a tailor in Katutura and a coffee roaster in the Netherlands earn revenue the same way: money comes in from sales before wages, rent or materials are paid.

### Revenue is not profit

Revenue and profit are different measures, and mixing them up ruins any judgement about a firm. Revenue counts the money coming in from sales; profit is what remains after the costs of production are paid, so profit is total revenue minus total cost. A firm can take a large revenue and still make a loss, where its costs are larger still; a firm with modest sales can be profitable where its costs are small. The two are told apart by asking what is being counted: sales income alone, or sales income set against the payments the firm makes.

## Working out revenue

### Two formulas and their units

Total revenue = price x quantity sold, and average revenue = total revenue / quantity sold. Each answer carries its unit, N$. Marula Foods sells 800 jars of marula spread at N$25 a jar: total revenue = N$25 x 800 jars = N$20 000, and average revenue = N$20 000 / 800 jars = N$25, which is the price itself where each jar sells at the same figure.

### A price cut, worked through

To win more sales the firm cuts its price to N$20 a jar and sells 1 100 jars. Total revenue = N$20 x 1 100 jars = N$22 000, above the N$20 000 earned before, and average revenue = N$22 000 / 1 100 = N$20, the new price. The change in total revenue is N$22 000 - N$20 000 = N$2 000: the 300 extra jars added N$20 x 300 = N$6 000 against the N$5 x 800 = N$4 000 given up on the jars it had been selling at the higher price.

### How sales influence revenue

Where the price holds steady, more sales raise total revenue in step: ten more jars at N$25 add N$25 x 10 = N$250. Where the firm must cut its price to win sales, the outcome turns on whether the extra units add more income than the price cut gives up - the cut from N$25 to N$20 gave up N$5 on each of the 800 old jars, and the 300 new jars more than covered it. A fishing firm in Portugal reading its own sales figures applies the same test before it cuts a price.

## What firms are trying to achieve

### Four aims

Firms may pursue survival, social welfare, profit maximisation and growth, and the aim that leads changes with the firm's situation. Profit is total revenue minus total cost, and profit maximisation is the aim of making that gap as large as the firm can. Growth means expanding the firm's sales, its premises or its share of the market. Survival means keeping the firm going through a hard stretch. Social welfare means running the firm for the good of the community around it.

### Which aim leads, where

A juice processor newly opened in Oshakati may put survival first: covering its costs and keeping its customers matters more to it than the largest possible profit while its name is still unknown. A secure firm, such as an established bottler in Brazil with steady sales, may choose growth - a second line, a new warehouse - accepting a smaller profit now for bigger sales later. A bus service run for a town council may put social welfare first, keeping fares low so workers reach their jobs, and a council water utility in Namibia is set up the same way.

### When aims pull apart

Objectives may pull against each other. Paying workers more, keeping fares low or funding a local clinic raises the firm's costs or trims its revenue, and each such step may shrink profit. A firm choosing growth spends on expansion rather than taking profit out. A firm set on survival may accept a loss for a season to keep its customers. So a firm often trades some profit away for another aim, and which trade it makes is a judgement about its situation rather than a fixed rule.
