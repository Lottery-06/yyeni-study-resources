# 1.1 The nature of the economic problem

*cie-0455-igcse-2026. Offline study notes, rendered from the approved content units on 2026-10-13. Everything here is also what the flashcards are built from.*

**What this topic asks of you**

- Explain the nature of the economic problem: finite resources against unlimited wants, and why it forces choice on consumers, workers, producers and governments.
- Define an economic good and a free good, and use the test that tells them apart in a context.

---

## Finite resources, unlimited wants

### The problem every economy shares

Wants are unlimited. A household in Katutura wants food, school fees, airtime and a fridge this month; the money is not there for all of them. A government wants clinics in every settlement, tarmac on every road and pensions paid; the tax revenue cannot stretch to all three at once. The resources available - money, time, labour, land, machines - are finite. Because wants outrun resources, choices must be made about what to use them on and what to leave undone. That situation is the economic problem, and every economy faces it, rich or poor. In Germany, a wealthy country, the same problem appears in a different dress: the health service cannot fund every new drug and every extra ward in the same year, so it too must choose.

### The same problem at every level

The economic problem is not only a problem of the poor. A consumer with N$500 must choose between transport and a textbook. A worker with one free Saturday must choose between overtime and family. A producer with one factory line must choose between juice bottles and cider bottles. A government with a fixed budget must choose between a new dam and new classrooms. In each case the resource is finite, the wants placed on it are unlimited, and a choice with a cost attached follows. This is why the economic problem is the starting point of the whole subject: every later topic is about how a society answers the question the problem forces - what to produce, how, and for whom.

### Connections

Because choice is forced, every choice has an opportunity cost, which topic 1.3 develops; and because resources are finite, an economy cannot have more of everything at once, which the production possibility curve of topic 1.4 shows as a boundary.

## Economic and free goods

### What makes a good economic or free

An economic good is scarce relative to wants: there is not enough for everyone who wants it at a zero price, so obtaining it requires a sacrifice, and a price is normally charged to ration it. A free good is the opposite: supply exceeds wants at a zero price, so it commands no price and no sacrifice is involved in taking it. Sunshine in the Namib is close to a free good - no one rations it and no one sells it. A 5 000-litre tanker of borehole water delivered to Windhoek is an economic good, because supplying it costs labour, fuel and pumps, and the N$180 charged is what rations the scarce delivery capacity.

### The test in use

The test that separates them is scarcity relative to wants, which shows up as an opportunity cost: does using the good mean giving something else up? Apply it to two goods a beach cafe in Lisbon sees: the seawater outside its door, and the bottled seawater spray it sells to kitchens at 14 euros a bottle. The sea itself is a free good - there is more than anyone wants, so taking some sacrifices nothing. The bottled spray is an economic good - the labour, bottles, labels and delivery in each one could have been used elsewhere, so it is scarce relative to wants and a price is charged. A price is a clue, not the test: state schooling is free to the pupil, yet the teachers, classrooms and books it uses could serve other purposes, so it is still an economic good. Note the nuance: the same substance can be a free good in one form and an economic good in another, because scarcity is a relationship between supply and wants, not a property of the substance.

### Connections

Free goods are not the same as public goods, which topic 2.10 meets as a reason markets under-provide; and the sacrifice involved in an economic good is the opportunity cost of topic 1.3.
