## The macroeconomic aims of government

### The four aims

The macroeconomic aims of government are the economy-wide outcomes a government works towards: low inflation, low unemployment, steady growth in the output of the economy, and a current account close to balance. They are macroeconomic because each describes the whole economy rather than one market: the general price level, not the price of one bag of maize meal; the share of the workforce without jobs across the country, not the staff of one shop in Katutura. A ministry in Windhoek and a ministry in Tokyo list the same four aims, because they describe conditions an economy can be in rather than policies one government favours. The aims are destinations; the policy tools come in topic 4.3.

### Reading the aims in figures

Each aim is read off an indicator. Low inflation is measured by the rate of inflation, the percentage rise in the general price level over a year. The jobs aim is measured by the unemployment rate, the percentage of the labour force without work but seeking it. Growth is measured by the annual percentage rise in real output of the whole economy, and external balance is read from the current account balance. So a learner in Walvis Bay reading that inflation slowed to 4.1%, unemployment stood at 12.6%, output grew by 3.0% and the current account deficit reached 3.4% of export value can say which aims the economy moved towards and which it missed.

### Whom each aim serves

Low inflation tends to protect the buying power of wages and savings, so a worker in Gobabis whose pay rises by 5.0% while prices rise by 3.0% is better off in real terms, and savers and people on fixed pensions hold their ground when price rises stay slow. Low unemployment raises household incomes, keeps skills from fading and lifts tax revenue while cutting support payments. Steady growth raises the output and average incomes the economy can sustain, where the gains are shared out across households. A current account close to balance means a country is not persistently spending more abroad than it earns, which steadies its repayments to foreign lenders. The importance of each aim varies with circumstances: where unemployment is high, jobs tend to lead the ranking; where prices are rising fast, inflation tends to lead.

## Conflicts between the aims

### What a conflict of aims is

A conflict between macroeconomic aims is a situation in which the conditions that serve one aim make another harder to achieve, so pushing towards one destination means moving away from another at the same time. The clearest case runs through how much the whole economy spends. When spending rises quickly across the economy, firms take on more workers and unemployment tends to fall, which serves the jobs aim. Where workplaces are near full, the same pressure tends to push prices up, straining the inflation aim, and part of the extra spending goes abroad on imports, widening a current account deficit. Chasing the growth aim hard in these conditions tends to pull the other aims out of reach.

### A conflict in numbers

One year spending across an economy grows fast. Its unemployment rate falls from 16.0% to 13.5%, which moves it towards the jobs aim. Its inflation rate rises from 3.8% to 6.9%, which moves it away from the inflation aim. Spending on imports grows by 9.0% while export earnings grow by 4.0%, so the current account moves towards a bigger deficit. Brazil's boom years showed the same shape: quick growth and low unemployment at first, then faster price rises and strain on the country's external accounts. The government reading these faces a ranking decision: easing the pressure on prices would mean cooling the spending that created the jobs.

### Cause or consequence?

The register of common errors records learners labelling a possible cause as a consequence, or the reverse, when a question asks about conflicts between aims. The boundary runs along the direction of the arrow: a quick rise in spending across the economy can be a cause of faster rises in prices; faster rises in prices are the consequence of it. The test is to ask whether the named item sets the change in motion or follows from it. If a figure or event starts the process, it is a cause; if it results from the process, it is a consequence. A learner who writes that price rises cause spending to grow has reversed a relationship that runs the other way in this analysis.
