## What the government does in an economy

### The role of government defined

The government is the authority that runs a country. In an economy its role is to provide public goods and services that firms tend not to find profitable to sell - street lighting in Oshakati lights everyone who passes, and buyers tend to wait for a neighbour to pay rather than paying themselves - to raise revenue through taxes on income and spending, to transfer some of that revenue to households through payments such as the old-age pension, to regulate the markets that firms and consumers trade in, and to manage the economy as a whole toward aims such as jobs and steady prices. The German city of Hamburg runs its water supply the same way: one public provider, one set of pipes, funded from public revenue.

### Why the government steps in

Why does the government do these jobs rather than leave them to markets? Start where a market works: two traders exchange because each gains. Notice what the market cannot do. Street lighting: because buyers who do not pay still enjoy the light, a firm selling it collects from a few and serves many, so providing it tends to be unprofitable. Dominance: where one seller grows so dominant that buyers lose choice, the seller may raise prices above what a competitive market would charge, so the government regulates. Judged-unfair outcomes: where income divides very unequally, a government that judges this unfair can redirect resources through taxes and pensions. So each government function traces to something the market alone does not deliver - which is why the role of government differs from country to country and from year to year.

### How far the role reaches

The role is wide, not unlimited. In Namibia the government runs schools, pays the old-age pension out of tax revenue, licenses banks and inspects taxis; in the Netherlands the government spends close to half the country's yearly output, while in a poorer country the same list is shorter. The boundary is not fixed. A very poor country may lack the revenue to pay pensions on a wide scale - revenue limits it. Taxes raised too high may push a business toward shutting or moving - the activity shrinks, and with it later tax revenue. Running an industry, as with a national airline, requires skill and information a government may lack, so it is likely to run some industries less well than a firm with market experience. So a government widens its role where it can raise revenue and run the task, and narrows it where it cannot - the role moves with capacity.

### The boundary in a real situation

In 2026 the Kavango river valley's small farms have no all-weather road to the Katima Mulilo market. Start where the market stands: no single trader gains enough from a road to build it, so none is built. Why the government may build it: every farm gains and shoppers in Katima Mulilo gain, so from public revenue the government builds what no one buyer would. How people react: if the money comes from a new levy on the same farms, some farmers say they would rather keep their earnings and wait; if a trucking firm lobbies for the levy to fall on its rival's goods, the rule may help the trucking firm while leaving shoppers worse off. The limit: where the valley's next project costs more than the region's whole revenue, the government cannot build it, and the want goes unmet. This example names every side of the role: provider, taxer, regulator, and the boundary of what is possible.
