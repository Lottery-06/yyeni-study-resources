## What money supply and monetary policy mean

### Money supply and the policy built on it

Money supply is the total stock of money in an economy at a point in time: the notes and coins in circulation plus the bank deposits that can be spent by card, cheque or transfer. Deposits count because they pay for goods in the same way cash does. Monetary policy is the use of changes in interest rates and of measures to control the growth of the money supply to influence the economy's performance. In Namibia the Bank of Namibia, the central bank, carries it out, setting the rate at which it lends to commercial banks, and that rate shapes the loan and deposit rates banks offer households and firms. Central banks are often confused with commercial banks: a commercial bank takes deposits and makes loans, while the central bank stands above the banks and steers them. The Bank of Japan plays this steering role, as does the central bank of the Netherlands within the European system.

### One rate rise traced through a bank

In 2024 Oshakati Savings Bank charges households 6% a year on loans. The Bank of Namibia raises the rate at which it lends to commercial banks, so the commercial bank is likely to raise its loan rate to 9%, because its own funds cost more. The interest on a N$20 000 loan at 6% is 0.06 x N$20 000 = N$1 200 a year. At 9% it is 0.09 x N$20 000 = N$1 800. The gap of N$1 800 - N$1 200 = N$600 is what a borrowing household feels, so households tend to borrow less and the stock of money grows more slowly. This is the lever monetary policy pulls.

## The measures of monetary policy

### The two main measures

The first measure is changing interest rates: the central bank raises or lowers the rate at which it lends to commercial banks, which tend to move their loan and deposit rates the same way. The second measure is controlling the growth of the money supply: the central bank can sell government securities to banks, which pay from their reserves and then hold less to lend, or tighten the rules under which banks create new deposits through lending.

### A rate rise traced

Suppose the policy rate rises from 4% to 8%. Etosha Gate Lodge plans a N$150 000 kitchen extension on a bank loan: at 4% the yearly interest is 0.04 x N$150 000 = N$6 000, at 8% it is 0.08 x N$150 000 = N$12 000, so the lodge is likely to postpone. Households move the same way - dearer credit, better-rewarded saving - so borrowing falls, fewer new deposits are created, and the growth of the money supply slows.

### Monetary measures or supply-side measures?

The boundary: a monetary measure works on the cost of borrowing and the stock of money; a supply-side measure works on the economy's capacity to produce. The test is to ask what the measure changes - the price of money, or what the economy can produce. A grant helping Kaoko Juice buy bottling equipment is supply-side; a policy-rate rise is monetary.

### Strength and drawbacks

Strength: interest-rate changes work through the whole banking system at once, reaching households and firms across the economy within days. Drawbacks: the effects arrive with a delay, spending financed without bank credit responds less, and dearer borrowing may hold back firms' building projects and jobs. A rate rise cools credit-financed spending faster than it cools a cash-only market stall, in Vietnam as in Namibia.

## Monetary policy and the government's aims

### Which aims monetary policy moves

Monetary policy bears most directly on price stability, the aim of keeping the rise in the general price level low and steady. It touches the other aims indirectly. Lower interest rates tend to encourage borrowing, spending and output growth, which supports jobs; higher rates tend to cool them. The external position is touched too, since interest rates move exchange rates, developed in topic 6.3. So one instrument is asked to serve several aims at once, and the aims may pull in different directions.

### A rate rise against a 9% rise in prices

Suppose prices rise 9% a year and the central bank lifts its policy rate from 5% to 9%. Borrowing dearer -> households borrow less and save more -> spending slows -> firms raise prices more slowly, so pressure on the aim of price stability eases. The chain runs on: Marula Foods in Gobabis is likely to postpone a cold-room built on a loan, so output grows more slowly and jobs weaken, against the aims of growth and employment. Higher rates may also draw funds from abroad, tending to lift the currency and make exports harder to sell - developed in topic 6.3.

### One lever, several aims, different households

The same rise lands differently. Savers gain: a deposit of N$10 000 earning 8% instead of 4% brings N$800 a year instead of N$400. Borrowers lose: a N$20 000 loan costs N$1 800 a year at 9% where it cost N$1 000 at 5%. Households on fixed money incomes gain when the rise in prices slows. If Brazil's central bank lifts its rate sharply to slow a rise in prices, it accepts slower growth as the price of steadier prices - the trade-off every central bank weighs.
