## Measuring living standards

### Living standards and the indicators that measure them

Living standards are the level of material well-being of a person or a household: how well the household is housed, fed, clothed, schooled and cared for, together with the security of that level. The starting measure is Gross Domestic Product - the total value of the goods and services produced in a country in a given period - but for living standards the figure to use is GDP per head, that total divided by the population: it shows the average output available per person, so countries of different sizes compare.

The indicators that complete the picture: income per person; health measures such as life expectancy and infant mortality; education measures such as mean years of schooling and literacy; and access to basic services such as clean water and electricity. Together they capture what GDP per head alone may miss, and they travel in company: where output per person rises, health and schooling tend to rise with it.

### Reading the figures for two countries

Namibia records GDP of N$150 billion and a population of 2.5 million: GDP per head is N$150 000m / 2.5m, which is N$60 000 a year. Vietnam, as a non-African example, records GDP of US$300 billion with 100 million people: GDP per head is US$3 000 a year, and at N$20 to US$1 that is N$60 000 as well, so the averages match. The other indicators: life expectancy 74 years in Vietnam against 60 in Namibia, household electricity 99% against about half. One figure is a doorway, not a verdict: the same GDP per head stands while health and access separate the two, because GDP per head is an average that may hide how income is shared.

### What GDP per head may hide

GDP per head is an average, so where income is spread unevenly the typical household may earn well below it: a mean of N$60 000 can stand beside a majority earning a fraction of that. Output may also include goods that change living standards little, so the health and access indicators travel further than the money figure.

## Comparing living standards and income distribution

### Comparing living standards honestly

To compare living standards between countries, GDP per head is first adjusted for the cost of living, because a money figure alone may mislead where prices differ: the same income buys more where food and rent are cheaper. The adjusted figure is then read alongside health, education and access indicators - life expectancy, schooling, clean water - which capture outcomes the money figure cannot.

The distribution of income is how a country's total income is shared among its households, and living standards depend not just on the average income a country produces but on where a household sits in that sharing. The Netherlands and Brazil illustrate: income is spread evenly in the Netherlands, while in Brazil a smaller group takes a larger share of a similar average, so the typical Dutch household tends to live better even where the averages sit close.

### Why the same average can house different lives

Start from two countries with the same GDP per head of N$60 000 a year, where the middle household takes about 70% of the average in one and about 40% in the other. The typical household earns about 0.70 x N$60 000 = N$42 000 a year in the evenly shared country against about 0.40 x N$60 000 = N$24 000 in the other. This causes what the typical household can afford - schooling, healthcare, housing - to diverge though the average is identical. Therefore a comparison stopping at GDP per head ranks the two as equal, while the distribution of income has decided the typical household in one lives better.

### Progressive does not mean regressive

The confusion: learners mix up a progressive income tax with a regressive income tax system, assuming both take a larger share from high earners, or judge a system by amounts paid rather than shares. The boundary: a progressive income tax takes a larger share of income from high earners, tending to narrow the gap in take-home pay; a regressive one takes a larger share from low earners, widening the gap. The test that tells them apart: which group pays the larger share of its own income - the shares, not the amounts.
