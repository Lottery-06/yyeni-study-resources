## What supply-side policy means

### The definition and its boundary

Supply-side policy is the use of government measures to raise the economy's capacity to produce, by making workers more productive, removing barriers that hold firms back, and strengthening the incentive to work, save and invest. The name carries the meaning: these measures act on the supply side, the side of the economy that makes goods and services. The boundary with the other families matters, because a question naming one family expects measures from it. Monetary policy, topic 4.4, works on the cost of borrowing and the stock of money. Fiscal policy, topic 4.3, works on government spending and taxation to steer total spending. Supply-side policy pulls a different lever: it does not steer this year's spending, it raises what the economy can produce in the years ahead.

### One measure traced through a firm

Take a supply-side measure in action. Kaoko Juice in Opuwo finds that many job applicants cannot read batch instructions, so it can hire 40 workers yet uses fewer of them on skilled tasks. A government training programme for school leavers changes this: output per trained worker rises from 200 bottles a day to 240 bottles a day, so 40 workers now produce 40 x 240 = 9 600 bottles a day where they produced 40 x 200 = 8 000 before. The firm's capacity to produce has risen with no new machines, which is the lever supply-side policy pulls. In the Netherlands, Germany and Portugal the same lever shows in apprenticeship systems that pair school with workplaces.

## The measures of supply-side policy

### The six measures

The syllabus names six measures. Education and training: spending on schools, colleges and workplace training to raise the skills of workers. Labour market reforms: changes to hiring rules and job placement that make it easier for firms to take on workers and for job seekers to find them. Lower direct taxes: cutting income tax and company tax so each hour worked and each Namibian dollar of profit kept pays better. Deregulation: removing rules that raise firms' costs or block new entrants. Improving incentives to work and invest: benefits and tax rules shaped so working and investing pay better. Privatisation: moving a state-owned business into private hands. Japan's apprenticeship reforms show the same measures abroad.

### Two measures traced

Trace a tax cut: the rate of income tax falls from 24% to 18% for a Katutura worker earning N$84 000 a year. Take-home pay rises from N$84 000 - 0.24 x N$84 000 = N$63 840 to N$84 000 - 0.18 x N$84 000 = N$68 880 a year. The reward from an extra hour of work rises, so the worker is more likely to offer overtime, and firms, keeping more profit, may fund new equipment. Trace deregulation: cutting a Windhoek registration from 9 permits and 40 days to 3 permits and 10 days lowers the cost of starting up, so more new firms are likely to enter.

### Strength, drawback and a caution on privatisation

Strength: measures such as training raise capacity at the root, and the gains persist for a working life, in Vietnam as in Namibia. Drawback: the measures act slowly, so a government facing a problem now tends to reach for other policies first; and the gains depend on execution, since a poorly taught course adds little and a tax cut may be saved rather than invested. On privatisation: a private owner tends to watch costs and customers more keenly, but where the state-owned firm had no rival, the new owner may simply inherit that same position, so the expected gains may go missing.

## Supply-side policy and the government's aims

### Which aims the measures move

The macroeconomic aims are those of topic 4.2: growth in total output, employment, price stability and a sound external position. Supply-side measures tend to serve them through one channel: the economy's capacity to produce. A more productive workforce tends to mean more total output, so the growth aim is served. Workers who produce more tend to be easier to employ profitably, so the employment aim gains. Where the economy can produce more for the same effort, pressure on prices tends to ease, and a leaner cost base tends to make exports easier to sell abroad, serving the external position.

### A training programme traced to the aims

Suppose the government funds a welding course in Otavi, and 200 trainees raise their output per worker by a fifth. Mechanism: each trained worker produces more, so firms in Otavi can fill larger orders without taking on more machines; total output rises, serving growth. Because the trained welders generate more revenue per hour, firms tend to offer more hours and take on more of them, serving employment. Where local firms can quote lower prices from lower costs per unit, buyers gain and pressure on prices eases, serving price stability. Against this, the course costs public money now, while its gains build over years - the trade-off the government weighs.

### One measure, several aims, uneven gains

The gains land unevenly. A worker who gains a skill earns more and keeps a job more easily. A firm gains capacity and may cut its cost per unit. The government tends to gain on the aims of growth and employment but pays the cost now, and where it cuts direct taxes to sharpen incentives, it loses revenue, which may force public spending down. Consumers gain where lower costs lead to slower price rises. In Brazil, a large training institute widened gains across whole regions, but trainees far from the institutes waited years.
