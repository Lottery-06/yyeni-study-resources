## How a market system works

### Buyers, sellers and prices

A market system is a way of organising an economy in which buyers and sellers interact in markets, and the prices emerging from that interaction decide how scarce resources are allocated. No official tells the fisherman at Walvis Bay how many kob to land; the buyers' price does that. A Tokyo shopper choosing between two rice brands does the work a planner would otherwise do.

### Equilibrium and disequilibrium at Otavi

At the weekend maize market at Otavi, sellers bring 300 bags and buyers want 300 at N$95 a bag: planned buying equals planned selling, so N$95 is the market equilibrium price. At N$70, buyers plan 380 bags but sellers bring 240, so 140 planned purchases go unsatisfied: a shortage, which is market disequilibrium. At N$120, sellers bring 340 bags but buyers plan 260, leaving 80 unsold: a surplus.

### Minimum price or maximum price?

Learners often mix up a minimum price with a maximum price because both are official limits on a price. The legal direction decides which is which: a minimum price is a floor that sellers may not charge below; a maximum price is a ceiling that sellers may not charge above. Where the limit sits against equilibrium decides whether it bites. In the Otavi example, a floor of N$120, above the N$95 equilibrium, tends to leave a surplus; a ceiling of N$70, below it, tends to leave a shortage. A ceiling of N$120 would change nothing, because the market already clears at N$95.

### How well does the market system allocate?

A market system tends to direct resources towards the goods buyers want most: a good scarce relative to wants fetches a higher price, and the higher price rewards those who supply it. Yet the system may direct resources poorly. Where buyers cannot be charged for a good - clean air over Walvis Bay - no price rewards provision, so the market tends to under-provide it; and where a price ignores costs imposed on others, a factory that dirties water may expand even where the harm outweighs its output. Its success therefore depends on whether a price exists that carries the good's full cost.

## What, how and for whom

### Three questions scarcity forces

Because resources are finite and wants are not, an economy cannot produce everything, so three key allocation decisions must be made. What to produce: maize meal or blueberries, clinics or football stadiums. How to produce it: by hand with many workers, or by machine with few; in Oshakati or in Vietnam. For whom to produce it: for the households with the money to pay, or spread more widely. An economy run by central officials answers these questions through government plans; a market economy answers them through prices and spending. Whichever system is used, the three questions wait to be answered, because scarcity does not pause for any system.

### Kaoko Juice faces the three decisions

Kaoko Juice in Opuwo has 20 workers, two pressing machines and N$80 000 for the season. What to produce: marula juice sells at N$28 a bottle against N$19 for orange, so the marula line gets the machines first. How to produce it: hand-pressing uses 12 workers and yields 400 bottles a day, machine-pressing uses 4 workers and yields 900 bottles, so the machines run while workers pick. For whom to produce it: supermarkets in Windhoek pay promptly, the stall at the Ruacana gate takes 60 bottles a week, so production is weighted towards the supermarkets. Notice how the decisions interlock: choosing marula (what) made machine-pressing worthwhile (how), and the output went where the money was (for whom).

### Why the decisions cannot be taken separately

The what decision narrows the how decision, because a chosen product limits which methods can make it. The how decision then sets the cost per bottle, and cost limits what can be offered at a price consumers will pay, which feeds the for whom decision. So an economy that changes one answer usually finds the other two shifting with it, and a producer who ignores the link may find its chosen product cannot be made cheaply enough for anyone it hoped to serve.

## The price mechanism answers

### Signals and incentives

The price mechanism is the way changing prices act as signals of scarcity and as incentives, moving resources between competing uses without any central planner directing them. A price that rises tells buyers the good has become scarcer relative to wants and tells producers that expanding output is more rewarding than before. A price that falls sends the opposite message on both sides. In the Netherlands, the nightly flower auctions at Aalsmeer move millions of stems between growers and buyers across Europe, and no official decides the split: the auction prices do, because each grower reads them and chooses what to plant next season.

### A shortage of tomatoes in Oshakati

Suppose tomato blight cuts the Oshakati supply while the town keeps growing. The market price moves from N$15 a kilogram to N$26. That higher price is a signal: it announces that tomatoes are scarcer relative to wants than before. It is also an incentive: growers around Oshakati now earn N$26 a kilogram instead of N$15, so more of them plant tomatoes, and some consumers switch to cabbage at N$9 a kilogram while the price is high. The rise of N$26 - N$15 = N$11 a kilogram is small next to the whole economy, yet it shifts land, water and labour towards tomatoes and shifts some buyers away - which is how a market answers a scarcity problem without being told to.

### How prices answer the three questions

The price mechanism answers the three allocation decisions. What to produce: goods whose prices and profits are rising attract resources, so the economy tends to produce more of what buyers are revealing they want. How to produce it: dearer labour relative to machines pushes producers towards the cheaper method for the same output. For whom to produce it: output flows towards the buyers able and willing to pay, so income decides whom the market serves. Each answer is steering, not commanding: prices lean on millions of separate decisions, and the sum of those decisions is the allocation.
