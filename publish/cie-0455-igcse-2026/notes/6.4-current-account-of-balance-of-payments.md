## The four parts and their balances

### What the account records

The current account records transactions between residents and non-residents over a period, such as one year. Its four sections are trade in goods, trade in services, primary income and secondary income. A receipt from abroad is recorded as a credit; a payment abroad is a debit. A section balance equals receipts minus payments. Add the four section balances to obtain the current-account balance: a positive result is a surplus and a negative result is a deficit.

### Putting transactions in the right section

Goods are physical products: a Namibian seller exporting dried fruit to Botswana earns a goods credit, while imported machinery is a goods debit. Services include transport and tourism: a German visitor buying accommodation from a Namibian lodge purchases a service export, even though the visitor consumes it in Namibia. Primary income includes earnings from work and investments across borders, such as interest received from a foreign deposit or dividends paid to overseas shareholders. Secondary income includes current transfers without a corresponding good, service or asset received in return, such as money a worker abroad sends to family at home. Buying shares is different from receiving a dividend on them; the dividend is primary income, whereas the asset purchase belongs outside the current account.

### Four balances, one total

Use these invented annual figures, all in N$ million. Goods receipts are 120 and payments 150, giving 120 - 150 = -30. Services receipts are 40 and payments 20, giving 40 - 20 = 20. Primary income receipts are 8 and payments 12, giving 8 - 12 = -4. Secondary income receipts are 10 and payments 6, giving 10 - 6 = 4. The current account is -30 + 20 - 4 + 4 = -10: a deficit of N$10 million. A services surplus therefore can offset part of a goods deficit without eliminating the overall deficit. Calculate each section before combining them, and retain the minus signs.

## Why deficits and surpluses arise

### The gap between receipts and payments

A current account deficit means current-account payments exceed receipts during the period. A surplus means receipts exceed payments. An imbalance can result from changes in any of the four components, so a goods-trade explanation may be incomplete. Income paid to overseas investors or a change in remittances can alter the balance even when goods exports and imports stay unchanged.

### Spending and competitiveness

When domestic incomes rise, households and firms may spend more on imports. If imports rise while other flows stay unchanged, payments increase and the current account worsens. Higher demand abroad may instead increase export receipts. Better quality, reliable delivery and lower production costs can make exports more attractive to overseas buyers, but the effect depends on buyers responding and producers filling orders. Relatively high domestic inflation can weaken price competitiveness where it raises export prices compared with rivals. A rise in the world price of an essential imported input can increase payments where quantity demanded does not fall enough to offset the higher price.

### Two possible sources of a change

In an invented example, a Namibian economy starts with a balanced current account. Orders for dried fruit from Botswana and Portugal fall by N$12 million in a year, while its other receipts and payments stay unchanged. Export receipts fall, so it develops a N$12 million deficit. In a different year, imports of drought-related food rise by N$8 million while other flows stay unchanged; higher payments cause an N$8 million deficit. The first change comes from weaker overseas demand and the second from greater import needs. Naming the cause matters because stronger marketing cannot directly remove a domestic food shortage.

### The budget is a different balance

The confusion is between the government budget and international transactions. A budget deficit compares government spending with government revenue; a current account deficit compares external current payments with receipts, and a surplus reverses that comparison. The test is to identify the flows. A government budget deficit and a current account surplus can coexist; one does not determine the other.

## Consequences for the economy

### Output and jobs

A deficit caused by consumers switching from home goods to imports can weaken domestic sales. Firms may cut production and employment, reducing domestic GDP. A surplus driven by stronger export orders can raise GDP and employment where firms have capacity to expand. A surplus caused by a recession reducing imports has a different meaning: it can accompany falling output and jobs. The sign of the balance alone does not reveal the condition of the economy.

### Currency and prices

Where the currency floats, extra import payments relative to export receipts can increase sales of home currency for foreign currency, putting downward pressure on its exchange rate, other currency flows unchanged. If depreciation follows, imported fuel and materials become dearer in home currency, which may raise firms' costs and consumer prices. Stronger export receipts can create upward currency pressure instead; appreciation may reduce import-price pressure but also weaken exporters' competitiveness. Investment flows can offset these pressures. With a fixed rate, the monetary authority may use foreign exchange reserves to support the currency. These reserves are foreign-currency assets available for external payments or currency-market intervention.

### What was imported?

Compare two invented situations. Namibian buyers switch N$20 million of annual purchases from local furniture to South African furniture while other flows stay unchanged. The resulting deficit is associated with lost orders for domestic firms, which may cut jobs. In another situation, a Namibian food processor imports N$20 million of equipment from Japan. Its initial import payment worsens the current account, but if the equipment raises productive capacity and future exports, output and jobs may rise later. The second deficit therefore need not indicate declining competitiveness. If it is financed by overseas borrowing, future interest payments may worsen primary income; the benefit depends on the equipment earning enough to meet those costs.

### A sustainable position

Persistent deficits can require borrowing, asset sales or reserve use; reserves cannot support repeated payments indefinitely. A large surplus can also reflect households being unable to afford imports. Judge the source, duration and financing of the imbalance before deciding whether it threatens living standards.

## Policies for external stability

### Stability rather than an exact zero

Balance of payments stability means a sustainable external payments position without persistent pressures forcing disruptive adjustment. A temporary deficit financing productive equipment can be manageable. Policy should address the cause of an imbalance rather than pursue a zero current-account balance in each period.

### Reducing or switching spending

Higher taxes, lower government spending or higher interest rates can restrain domestic spending and reduce import purchases. However, domestic firms can also lose sales, reducing GDP and jobs. Tariffs or quotas may switch expenditure to domestic substitutes, but they can raise household prices and costs for firms using imported inputs. Trading partners may retaliate, damaging exports. The benefit depends on suitable home substitutes being available.

### Changing relative prices

A depreciation can make exports cheaper in foreign currency and imports dearer at home, with quoted prices unchanged. An improvement in the current account depends on how price-elastic demand for exports and imports is: combined export and import demand must be elastic enough to respond to the price changes, and domestic supply must be able to expand. If essential imports have few substitutes or export capacity is constrained, higher import costs may outweigh additional export receipts. Contracts and production delays can slow the response.

### Improving the ability to export

In an invented case, a Namibian fruit processor loses Botswana and German orders because deliveries arrive late. Better transport infrastructure and worker training may cut delays and waste, helping the firm retain buyers and earn export receipts. Simply restricting imports would not repair late delivery and might make imported packaging dearer. A Japanese producer facing the same delivery problem could also benefit from better logistics. Such supply-side measures take time and public resources; they improve the external position if firms use them to produce goods buyers want.

### Choosing against the cause

For an import surge driven by excessive domestic spending, spending restraint may work quickly but has employment costs. For weak export quality or delivery, training and infrastructure address the cause more directly. On balance, combine measures where several causes operate, judging sustainable receipts against household welfare, public cost and adjustment time. A policy that closes a deficit by creating a deep recession can be a poor choice.
