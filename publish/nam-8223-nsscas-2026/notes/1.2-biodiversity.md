## Biodiversity

### Ecosystem, habitat and niche

An ecosystem is a community of living organisms together with the non-living, or abiotic, parts of the environment in which they live, all interacting with one another. A waterhole in Etosha is an example. The plants, zebra, insects, birds and microorganisms are the living parts. The water, soil, rock, temperature and light are the non-living parts. The living organisms depend on the non-living parts and on each other, for instance through feeding.

A habitat is the place where an organism lives. Many species can share one habitat, so the habitat does not describe what any one of them does. A niche is the role of an organism in its ecosystem. It includes the conditions the organism tolerates, such as the range of temperature or humidity, the resources it uses, such as its food and shelter, and its interactions with other organisms, such as being eaten by a predator or competing with another species for food. Two species can share a habitat and still have different niches. A plains zebra and a black rhinoceros both live on the same savanna, but the zebra feeds mainly on grass, whereas the rhinoceros feeds mainly on the leaves and shoots of shrubs. Their feeding relationships differ, so their niches differ.

### A niche is not a place

A common error is to say that the niche of an organism is where it lives. That is its habitat. A second error is to describe a niche only by the place, leaving out the feeding relationships. The test is to ask whether the answer names a place, which is the habitat, or what the organism does and needs in that place, which is the niche. A good statement of a niche says something about the conditions tolerated, the resources used and the other organisms involved, including what eats the organism and what it eats.

### Three levels of biodiversity

Biodiversity is the variety of living organisms in an area. It can be assessed at three levels.

The first level is the number and range of different ecosystems and habitats. A conservancy that contains riverine woodland, mopane woodland, open grassland and a seasonal pan has a wider range of habitats than one that is entirely grassland.

The second level is the number of species and their relative abundance. Relative abundance is the share of the individuals that each species makes up. A sample with many species, each represented by a fair number of individuals, has high species diversity. A sample dominated by one species, with the others represented by one or two individuals, has lower species diversity, even if it holds the same number of species. The total number of individuals does not measure diversity. A sample of 500 individuals of one species is less diverse than a sample of 40 individuals from eight species.

The third level is the genetic variation within each species. Individuals of one species carry different alleles of many genes, and a species or population with many alleles has greater genetic diversity than one with few.

### Why each level matters

A wide range of habitats tends to support more species. Each habitat offers different conditions and resources, so more niches are available, and species with different requirements can live in the area without competing for the same resources.

Greater genetic diversity tends to help a population survive change. Suppose a new disease reaches a population in which several alleles of a gene are present. It is more likely that some individuals carry alleles that give resistance. Those individuals tend to survive and reproduce, and they pass the alleles on. A small population founded from a few individuals tends to carry fewer alleles, so it is at greater risk. This holds when the alleles involved affect survival under the new conditions. More alleles do not guarantee survival.

### Random sampling

It is rarely possible to count every organism in an area, so a biologist takes samples and uses them to estimate the whole. A sample is useful when it is representative of the area. Random sampling means that every position in the study area has an equal chance of being chosen, and that chance makes the choice, not the investigator. One method is to lay two tape measures at right angles along two edges of the study area, produce two numbers from a random number table or a calculator, use them as coordinates, and place the quadrat at that point. The process is then repeated.

Random sampling matters for three reasons. It removes bias. An investigator choosing positions by eye tends to pick places with more conspicuous or attractive plants, which would overestimate abundance and diversity. It makes the sample more likely to be representative of the whole area, so the estimate is closer to the true value. It also allows statistical tests to be used, because those tests assume that positions were chosen by chance.

Random sampling does not make an estimate exact. The estimate tends to improve as the number of samples increases. Where conditions change steadily across an area, such as from a river bank to dry ground, random placement hides that change, and a transect is used instead.

### Simpson's Index of Diversity

Simpson's Index of Diversity, D, takes account of both the number of species and their relative abundance. The formula is D = 1 − Σ(n/N)², where n is the number of individuals of one species, N is the total number of individuals of all species in the sample, and Σ means the sum of the values for all the species. In the examination the formula is provided, but the learner must know what each symbol stands for.

The steps are as follows. For each species divide n by N. Square each result. Add the squares together. Subtract the total from 1. D is a ratio, so it has no unit.

### Worked example: a grassland sample

A team samples grassland near Rundu and counts 20 individuals of grass species P, 15 of species Q, 10 of species R and 5 of species S. First, N = 20 + 15 + 10 + 5 = 50. Next, n ÷ N for each species: 20 ÷ 50 = 0.4, 15 ÷ 50 = 0.3, 10 ÷ 50 = 0.2 and 5 ÷ 50 = 0.1. Squaring gives 0.16, 0.09, 0.04 and 0.01, and their sum is 0.30. Finally, D = 1 − 0.30 = 0.70.

### What the value of D means

D lies between 0 and 1. A value closer to 1 means higher diversity: many species, with the individuals spread fairly evenly between them. A value closer to 0 means lower diversity: one or a few species make up most of the individuals.

Three samples show the range. A sample of 100 individuals with 99 of one species and 1 of another gives (0.99)² + (0.01)² = 0.9801 + 0.0001 = 0.9802, so D = 1 − 0.9802 = 0.0198, which is close to 0. A sample with 50 of each of two species gives 0.25 + 0.25 = 0.5, so D = 0.5. A sample with 25 of each of four species gives 4 × 0.0625 = 0.25, so D = 0.75.

A community with a high D tends to be more resilient to change. A disease or change in conditions that harms one species affects a smaller share of the individuals. In a community with a low D, the dominant species makes up most of the individuals, so harm to that species affects most of the community. D is not a count of species, and comparing two areas is fair when they were sampled by the same method.

### Frame quadrats, line transects and belt transects

A frame quadrat is a frame of known area, such as 0.25 m² or 1 m², placed on the ground to mark out a sample. In each quadrat the investigator can count the individuals of a species, which gives abundance, estimate the percentage of the area covered by a species, or record whether the species is present, which gives frequency over many quadrats. Quadrats suit plants and animals that are fixed or slow-moving. The quadrat marks out an area but does not say where to put it. Where the area is fairly uniform, positions are chosen at random.

A line transect is a tape or string stretched across the area along a line chosen on purpose to cross a gradient. The organisms touching the line, or lying at set points along it, are recorded. A belt transect is a strip of known width, such as 1 m, along the line. It is sampled with a quadrat moved along the strip, either continuously or at regular intervals. A belt transect samples an area, so it gives more data than a line transect.

A transect is not a quadrat. The transect is the line along which samples are taken, and the quadrat is the frame that may be used along it. Transect positions are chosen deliberately to show change, for example with distance from a waterhole, so a transect is not random sampling.

### Mark-release-recapture and the Lincoln index

Quadrats do not suit animals that move away. For these, mark-release-recapture is used. A first sample of animals is caught, marked in a way that does not harm them or make them easier to catch, counted, and released. Call the number marked M. After enough time for them to mix with the rest of the population, a second sample is caught. Call its size N, and the number of marked animals in it R. The method assumes that the proportion of marked animals in the second sample equals the proportion of marked animals in the whole population: R ÷ N = M ÷ population. Rearranging, the estimate is given by the Lincoln index.

population estimate = (M × N) ÷ R

The formula is not supplied in the Mathematical requirements annexe, so it has to be learned. Example: 40 striped mice are caught in a conservancy, marked and released. The next day 30 are caught, and 6 of them are marked. The estimate is (40 × 30) ÷ 6 = 200 mice.

The estimate is reliable when the marked animals mix at random with the rest, when there is little migration, birth or death between the samples, when marking does not change the chance of capture, and when the marks are not lost. Individuals are counted in whole numbers, so an answer such as 145.83 is rounded to 146.

### Investigating distribution and abundance locally

Distribution is where a species is found, and abundance is how many individuals there are. To investigate a plant species on school grounds or a communal pasture, work in this order. First decide the question and the method. Mark out the study area and measure its size. Choose the positions of the quadrats by random coordinates, or set a transect across the gradient if the question is about change. Count the individuals in each quadrat. Repeat at many positions, then calculate the mean number per quadrat. Finally scale up: estimated total = mean per quadrat × (study area ÷ quadrat area).

Example: 10 quadrats of 0.25 m² on a plot of 60 m² give a mean of 4 grass tufts per quadrat. The plot holds 60 ÷ 0.25 = 240 quadrat areas, so the estimate is 240 × 4 = 960 tufts.

The positions are repeated because a single quadrat may by chance hold few or many individuals. A mean over many quadrats reduces the effect of chance and of any unusual quadrat. When animals are handled, they are treated gently and released where they were caught, and the habitat is left undamaged.

### Whole numbers, grazers and anomalous results

Three errors recur in local investigations. The first is giving a population estimate as a fraction or decimal. Individuals are counted in whole numbers, so the estimate is rounded to a whole number.

The second is writing that an animal feeds on grazers or browsers. Grazer and browser name the animal, not its food. A grazer feeds mainly on grass. A browser feeds mainly on the leaves and shoots of shrubs and trees. A plains zebra is a grazer and a black rhinoceros is a browser.

The third is dealing with an anomalous result by acting on the animals, for example by culling them or giving them contraception. An anomalous result is a value that does not fit the pattern of the rest. It is dealt with in the data: check for a counting or recording error, repeat the count if possible, and if the value is left out of the mean, say so and give the reason. The test is whether the action is applied to the data or to the animals.
