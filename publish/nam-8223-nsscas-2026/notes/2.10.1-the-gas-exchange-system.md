## The gas exchange system

### The route air takes and where the lungs lie

The gas exchange system is the set of tubes and air sacs that bring air close to the blood. Air enters through the nose or mouth and passes down the pharynx and the larynx into the trachea, the windpipe. The trachea runs down the neck into the chest, where it divides into two bronchi (singular bronchus), one entering each lung.

Inside a lung each bronchus branches into narrower bronchi and then into bronchioles, the finest air tubes. Each bronchiole ends in a cluster of tiny air sacs called alveoli (singular alveolus). Oxygen enters the blood and carbon dioxide leaves it at the alveoli, so the tubes above them exist to carry air to the alveoli, to clean it and to stay open. The order is therefore trachea, bronchi, bronchioles, alveoli.

The lungs lie in the thorax, the chest cavity, protected by the ribcage, and the diaphragm, a sheet of muscle, forms the floor of the cavity. Each lung is enclosed by two thin pleural membranes, one on the lung surface and one lining the chest wall. Between them is a thin film of pleural fluid, which lets the lung surface slide against the chest wall without friction as the lungs expand and recoil.

The two lungs together contain roughly 300 million to 500 million alveoli. Each is wrapped in a network of capillaries, the narrowest blood vessels. Together the alveoli give a gas exchange surface of roughly 70 m², a very large surface area folded into the space of the chest.

### Examining the gross structure of a sheep's lungs

Gross structure means what can be seen without a microscope. A sheep pluck is the heart, lungs and trachea taken together, and a butcher can supply one. Raw tissue can carry microbes, so wear gloves, work on a tray, cut away from your body, and wash and disinfect your hands, the tray and the instruments afterwards.

First look at the outside. The trachea feels ridged and firm, because of the cartilage in its wall. The surface of each lung is smooth and shiny, which is the pleural membrane, and the tissue underneath feels soft and spongy. Next use scissors to cut along the length of the trachea and open it out, so that you can see the moist lining and the pale bands of cartilage in the wall. Then follow the trachea down to the place where it divides into two bronchi, and cut along one bronchus into the lung to see it divide into smaller tubes. Finally cut a thin slice of lung and look for the many small air spaces.

Two further observations are worth making. A piece of fresh lung floats in water, because its alveoli and airways hold air, which makes the piece less dense than water. A piece that has been squeezed or has taken in water may float low or sink, so use fresh tissue. If a clean pump or tube fits the trachea, the lungs can be inflated gently. They stretch as air enters and shrink back when released, which shows that elastic tissue is present. Record what you see in a labelled drawing, and note any damage to the specimen, because a cut lung may not inflate properly.

### Where each tissue is found

A tissue is a group of similar cells that work together. An epithelium is a tissue of closely packed cells, in one or more layers, that covers a surface or lines a tube or cavity. The airways and alveoli are lined by epithelium, and other tissues lie in their walls.

Cartilage in the trachea is C-shaped rings, with the open part of each C at the back, against the oesophagus. In the bronchi it is irregular plates, which are smaller and fewer in the smaller bronchi. Cartilage is absent from the bronchioles and the alveoli.

Ciliated epithelium lines the trachea, the bronchi and the larger bronchioles. Goblet cells lie among the ciliated cells in the trachea and bronchi and become fewer in the bronchioles. Mucous glands lie in the wall beneath the lining of the trachea and bronchi. The alveoli have neither ciliated cells nor goblet cells.

Smooth muscle joins the open ends of the C-shaped cartilage at the back of the trachea, and forms a layer in the walls of the bronchi and bronchioles. The layer is relatively thicker in the bronchioles, where there is no cartilage. Elastic fibres lie in the walls of the trachea, bronchi, bronchioles and alveoli.

Squamous epithelium is an epithelium made of a single layer of very flat cells. It forms the wall of each alveolus. A dense network of capillaries surrounds each alveolus, so the alveolar wall and the capillary wall lie against each other.

### Recognising the tissues in a slide or micrograph

A photomicrograph is a photograph taken through a light microscope; an electron micrograph is an image made with an electron microscope, which shows much finer detail. In either, identify a tissue by the shape of its cells, what lies between them and where it sits in the section.

Cartilage has a smooth, glassy-looking pale matrix with scattered rounded cells, each sitting in its own small space. Smooth muscle is made of long, spindle-shaped cells, each with one nucleus in the middle and no stripes, packed in bundles. In a cut across a bundle the cells look like round profiles of different sizes, and some show no nucleus because the cut missed it.

Ciliated epithelium is a layer of tall, column-shaped cells with hair-like cilia on the free surface next to the lumen, and nuclei in a line near the base. Goblet cells between them are paler and swollen. 

A capillary in section is a small round or oval space about 7 µm wide, just wide enough for a red blood cell, with a wall one flat cell thick. Red blood cells are often visible inside. Squamous epithelium of an alveolus is a very thin line of flat cells with flattened nuclei, beside an air space. In an electron micrograph the alveolar wall and the capillary wall together measure less than 1 µm.

### Recognising the airways and drawing plan diagrams

Use the wall to name the airway, and check the size of the lumen against the layers in the wall.

In transverse section the trachea has a large round lumen with a flat back. Its wall contains a single C-shaped ring of cartilage, with a band of smooth muscle across the gap at the flat back. The lining is ciliated epithelium with goblet cells, and mucous glands lie in the wall beneath it.

A bronchus has a smaller lumen. Its cartilage is separate irregular plates around the lumen, not one C, and a layer of smooth muscle lies inside the plates. It has the same lining and mucous glands as the trachea.

A bronchiole has a small lumen and no cartilage and no mucous glands. The lining is thin, with few or no goblet cells, and the layer of smooth muscle often makes the lining look folded. Alveoli appear as many thin-walled air spaces packed together like a honeycomb. Each wall is one flat cell thick and contains capillaries, and there is no cartilage or smooth muscle.

A plan diagram shows where the tissues lie. Draw the outlines of the layers with clear, continuous lines and do not draw individual cells. Give the drawing a title and the magnification, and label the parts with ruled lines that do not cross. For the trachea draw and label the lumen, the lining, the glands, the cartilage and the muscle in their correct positions. For a bronchus, draw the cartilage as separate plates. Using the microscope and making the drawing are skills assessed at the bench; this section teaches what to look for and what the drawing must show.

### How the lining keeps the airways healthy

The air that is breathed in carries dust particles and microbes. If they reached the alveoli they could damage the thin wall or cause infection, so the lining of the airways removes many of them first.

Goblet cells, which lie among the ciliated cells, and mucous glands, which lie in the wall beneath the lining, secrete mucus onto the surface of the airways. Mucus is sticky, so dust particles and microbes that touch it are trapped.

The ciliated epithelial cells have cilia on the free surface. The cilia beat in a coordinated way, and this moves the layer of mucus, with the dust and microbes trapped in it, along the airway towards the throat. There the mucus is swallowed, where the acid in the stomach tends to kill many microbes, or is coughed out. The result is that fewer microbes and particles reach the alveoli.

The chain is therefore: goblet cells and mucous glands make mucus, the mucus traps the particles, and the beating cilia move the mucus along. If the cilia stopped beating while mucus was still being secreted, trapped dust and microbes would tend to stay in the airway for longer.

### Mucus traps; cilia move

A common error is to say that mucus sweeps dust out of the airway. This confuses two structures. Mucus does not move itself: it is a sticky fluid that traps dust and microbes. The beating cilia of the ciliated epithelial cells are what move the mucus along. A quick test is to ask which one moves and which one traps. The cilia move; the mucus traps. A full answer names both parts, in that order.

### What cartilage, smooth muscle, elastic fibres and squamous epithelium do

Cartilage supports the wall of the trachea and the bronchi and holds them open. When a person breathes in, the air pressure inside the airway falls, and without support a soft tube could collapse and block the flow. The gap in each C-shape, closed by smooth muscle, lets the oesophagus behind the trachea bulge slightly as food is swallowed.

Smooth muscle in the airway wall changes the width of the lumen. When it contracts, the lumen narrows and the flow of air is reduced. When it relaxes, the lumen widens and more air can flow. This matters most in the bronchioles, where there is no cartilage.

Elastic fibres lie in the walls of the airways and the alveoli. When a person breathes in, air enters the alveoli and the fibres stretch. When the person breathes out, the fibres recoil, which returns the alveoli to their smaller size and helps push air out.

Squamous epithelium forms the thin wall of the alveoli. Because the wall is one flat cell thick, and the capillary wall beside it is also one flat cell thick, oxygen and carbon dioxide have a very short distance to diffuse. A short distance means a fast exchange.

### Elastic fibres stretch and recoil; they do not hold alveoli open

A common error is to say that elastic fibres stop the alveoli collapsing. The alveoli are held open by the air inside them, not by the fibres. The fibres do something different: they stretch when air enters on breathing in and recoil on breathing out. The test is to ask what the fibres do on inhaling and on exhaling. The answer is stretch on inhaling and recoil on exhaling, and the recoil helps to push air out.

### Gas exchange between the alveoli and the blood

Gas exchange is the movement of oxygen from the air in the alveoli into the blood, and of carbon dioxide from the blood into the air in the alveoli. Both gases move by diffusion, the net movement of molecules from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration, down a concentration gradient.

Blood arriving at the alveoli has a lower concentration of oxygen and a higher concentration of carbon dioxide than the air in the alveoli. First, oxygen dissolves in the thin film of moisture that lines each alveolus. Then it diffuses down its concentration gradient across the alveolar wall, one flat cell thick, and across the capillary wall, also one flat cell thick, into the blood. Finally it enters the red blood cells. Carbon dioxide moves the other way: it diffuses from the blood, where its concentration is higher, across the same two walls into the air in the alveoli, and it is breathed out.

The gradients stay steep because both sides are refreshed. Breathing replaces the air in the alveoli with fresh air, which keeps the oxygen concentration there higher than in the blood and removes carbon dioxide. Blood flowing through the capillaries carries oxygen away and brings more carbon dioxide, so blood at the alveolus has a lower oxygen concentration than the alveolar air.

Four features speed the exchange: a very large surface area, a very short diffusion distance, a moist surface, and a dense capillary network that keeps the blood moving past.

### Worked example: naming an airway and explaining its wall

A class at a school in Rundu is told that a slide shows a transverse section through one airway of a goat's lung. A description of the slide reads: the lumen is small; the lining is folded; there is a thin layer of smooth muscle outside the lining; there is no cartilage and there are no glands; clusters of thin-walled air spaces lie against the outer edge.

Step one: check for cartilage. None is present, so the tube is not the trachea or a bronchus. Step two: check the other layers. A folded lining and a layer of smooth muscle with no glands fits a bronchiole. Step three: the clusters of thin-walled air spaces beside it are alveoli, which is what a bronchiole ends in. The airway is a bronchiole.

Now explain the wall. With no cartilage, the smooth muscle controls the width of the lumen: when it contracts, the lumen narrows and the folds deepen, and when it relaxes, the lumen widens. The thin lining carries few or no goblet cells, so little mucus is made here, and the cells that do line it can still move mucus towards the larger airways.
