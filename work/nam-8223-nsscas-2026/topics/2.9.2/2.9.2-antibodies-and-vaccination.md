## Antibodies and vaccination

### The structure of an antibody

An antibody is a protein made and secreted by plasma cells. It is a molecule, not a cell, and it is built from amino acids like any other protein. Each antibody binds one specific antigen. The levels of protein structure are taught in 2.4.2, and where plasma cells come from is taught in 2.9.1.

An antibody molecule is Y-shaped and has a quaternary structure, because it is made of four polypeptide chains. Two chains are long and identical, and are called heavy chains. Two are shorter and identical, and are called light chains. Disulfide bonds join each light chain to a heavy chain and join the two heavy chains to each other. A disulfide bond is a strong covalent bond between the sulfur atoms of two cysteine R groups. Within each chain, hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds and hydrophobic interactions hold the folded shape, as in other proteins. The two are not the same: disulfide bonds link separate chains, whereas the weaker bonds fold each chain.

At the tip of each arm of the Y, the end of a heavy chain and the end of a light chain lie side by side and form a variable region. In a variable region the sequence of amino acids differs from one antibody to another, so the folded tertiary shape differs too, and that shape makes an antigen-binding site. The molecule therefore has two antigen-binding sites, one on each arm, and the two sites are identical. The rest of the molecule, the stem and the lower parts of the arms, is the constant region. In antibodies of one type its sequence is the same whatever antigen they bind. A flexible stretch between the arms and the stem lets the arms swing, so that the two sites can reach antigens at different distances.

### How the structure fits the functions

The variable region explains why an antibody is specific. The binding site has a shape complementary to one antigen, rather like a lock that fits one key, and the antigen is held by many weak bonds that form where the two surfaces match closely. An antigen of a different shape fits poorly, forms few bonds and is not held. Because the body meets a great many different antigens, it makes a great many different antibodies, each with its own variable region and each made by its own clone of plasma cells.

Binding is what an antibody does, and three functions follow from it. First, an antibody can neutralise: bound to a toxin, or to the protein a virus uses to attach to a cell, it blocks that molecule from acting. Second, antibodies can agglutinate pathogens. Because each molecule has two binding sites, it can join two bacteria, and many antibodies link many bacteria into a clump that phagocytes can engulf in one go. Third, antibodies can mark pathogens for destruction. The constant region of a bound antibody is recognised by receptors on a phagocyte, so a pathogen coated in antibody is engulfed more readily than a bare one.

When you relate structure to function, name the feature and say what it allows. The variable regions allow specific binding, the two binding sites allow agglutination, and the constant region allows a phagocyte to recognise the coated pathogen.

### Monoclonal antibodies

During an infection, many clones of B-lymphocytes respond to the many different antigens, and to different parts of one antigen, on a pathogen. The blood therefore holds a mixture of antibodies. Monoclonal antibodies are different: they are identical antibodies, all made by a single clone of cells. They have the same variable regions, so they bind the same part of the same antigen.

A scientist who wants a large supply of one antibody meets a difficulty. The plasma cell that makes the wanted antibody divides only a limited number of times in a culture dish and then dies. The hybridoma method gets round this.

### The hybridoma method

The hybridoma method is outlined here as a sequence of steps, in order.

First, a mouse is injected with the antigen against which the antibody is wanted, for example a protein from a pathogen. B-lymphocytes in the mouse that have matching receptors are stimulated, and some become plasma cells. Second, B-lymphocytes that make antibody against the antigen are removed from the mouse's spleen. Third, these cells are mixed with myeloma cells, which are tumour cells that divide again and again, under conditions in which some of the cells fuse in pairs. Each fused cell is a hybridoma cell. Fourth, the hybridoma cells are separated from the cells that did not fuse and tested, so that the ones making the wanted antibody are selected, and each selected cell is grown into a clone. Fifth, the selected hybridoma cells are grown in large quantity in culture, and the antibody is collected from the culture fluid.

The fusion matters because the two parent cells each supply what the other lacks. The B-lymphocyte makes the wanted antibody but divides only a limited number of times in culture. The myeloma cell divides again and again but makes no antibody against the antigen. The hybridoma cell does both, so a clone of it gives a large and continuing supply of one antibody.

### Monoclonal antibodies in diagnosis and treatment

In diagnosis, a monoclonal antibody that binds one antigen is given a label, such as an enzyme that causes a colour change or a coloured particle. If the antigen is in the sample, the antibody binds it and the label shows the result. A pregnancy test works in this way: the antibody binds the hormone hCG in urine, and a coloured line appears. A rapid malaria test used in a clinic in northern Namibia is similar. The antibody binds a protein made by the malaria parasite in a drop of blood, and the result is ready in minutes, without a laboratory.

In treatment, the target is an antigen found in much greater amount on cancer cells than on most healthy cells. A monoclonal antibody is made against it and a drug or a radioactive substance is attached. When the antibody is injected it binds the cancer cells, so the drug is delivered to them, and fewer healthy cells are exposed than when the drug circulates freely. Some healthy cells carry a little of the antigen, so side effects are reduced rather than removed. The antibody can also act on its own, by blocking a signal that the cancer cell needs or by marking the cell for destruction.

Both uses depend on the same two features. Each antibody binds one specific antigen, so the test or the drug is directed at one target. And a hybridoma clone can be grown to give a large, steady and identical supply.

### Active and passive, natural and artificial immunity

In active immunity, the person's own immune system meets an antigen and the person's own plasma cells make the antibodies. Memory cells form as well. The first time, protection takes days to weeks to appear, and it is long-lasting because of the memory cells.

In passive immunity, the person receives ready-made antibodies from another source. The person's own plasma cells are not stimulated, so no memory cells form. Protection begins at once. It fades over weeks to months, because antibodies are proteins that are gradually broken down and are not replaced.

Natural and artificial are a separate pair of words. Natural immunity is gained in the course of normal life, without a medical procedure. Artificial immunity is gained through a deliberate medical procedure. Putting the two pairs together gives four cases. Natural active immunity follows an infection and recovery. Natural passive immunity is the transfer of antibodies from a mother to her child across the placenta before birth and in breast milk afterwards. Artificial active immunity follows vaccination. Artificial passive immunity follows an injection of ready-made antibodies, such as antivenom given after a snakebite, which is made from the antibodies of an animal such as a horse that has been given small doses of the venom.

The two pairs answer different questions. Active or passive asks who made the antibodies. Natural or artificial asks whether a medical procedure was involved. A vaccine is therefore artificial and active, and breast milk is natural and passive. You cannot work out one pair from the other.

### Vaccines and long-term immunity

A vaccine is a preparation of antigens from a pathogen, in a form that cannot cause the disease. The pathogen may be killed or inactivated, it may be a weakened live pathogen, or the vaccine may hold a purified antigen or an inactivated toxin. A vaccine does not contain ready-made antibodies. It contains antigens, and the recipient's own immune system does the work, which is why vaccination gives artificial active immunity.

The response is the primary response described in 2.9.1, started without the person being ill. Macrophages engulf the antigens and present them. A T-helper cell with a matching receptor activates a B-lymphocyte with a matching receptor, and the B-lymphocyte divides into plasma cells, which secrete antibody, and memory cells.

Memory cells persist for years, and they give long-term immunity. When the real pathogen later enters the body, the memory cells divide quickly into plasma cells. This secondary response is faster and larger than the first, so the pathogen is destroyed before it causes symptoms. Some vaccines are given in more than one dose, because a booster dose raises the number of memory cells. How long protection lasts differs between vaccines.

### Vaccination programmes and the control of disease

A vaccination programme gives a vaccine to a large part of a population. Its aim is herd immunity: a large enough proportion of the population is immune that an infected person passes the pathogen on to fewer than one other person on average. Most of that person's contacts are immune, so the chain of infection breaks, and the outbreak dies out. People who are not immune, such as very young infants and people whose immune systems are weak, are protected because the pathogen reaches them less often.

The proportion that must be immune depends on how easily the pathogen spreads. For measles, which spreads very easily, it is about 95 per cent of the population. It is lower for diseases that spread less easily. The level has to be kept up, because new babies who are not immune are born each year. If uptake falls well below it, outbreaks can return.

A programme works through a routine schedule of doses at clinics, outreach teams that reach remote communities and schools, a cold chain that keeps vaccines at a safe temperature, and records that show which children were missed. Control is easier when the pathogen's antigens change little and humans are its only host. Smallpox met both conditions and was eradicated by vaccination. The influenza virus changes its antigens, so its vaccines are updated.

### Errors to avoid

The first error is to call an antibody a cell, or to say that a B-lymphocyte clones into antibodies. Plasma cells make antibodies, and an antibody is a protein molecule. A test: ask which cell makes antibodies, and what antibodies are made of.

The second error is to say that a vaccine contains antibodies. It contains antigens. A test: ask who makes the antibodies, the recipient or another source. If it is the recipient, the immunity is active.

The third error is to read active and passive from natural and artificial, or the reverse. Vaccination is artificial, and it is active. A test: ask two separate questions, who made the antibodies and whether a medical procedure was involved.

### Reading antibody data

Graphs of antibody concentration in the blood show the difference between active and passive immunity. After an injection of ready-made antibodies the concentration is high at once, because the antibodies were made elsewhere, and it then falls steadily, because the antibodies are broken down. After a vaccine the concentration is close to zero at first, because the person's plasma cells have not yet responded, then rises over days to weeks to a peak, and falls slowly afterwards but stays above zero. If the real pathogen enters later, the concentration rises sooner and higher than after the vaccine, which shows the secondary response.

When you describe such a graph, quote values from it, name the time at which each change happens, and say what the shape means: an immediate high start means passive, a delay followed by a rise means active.

### Worked example: two children in Ohangwena

Two children live in Ohangwena Region. Hileni, who is 3 months old, has antibodies against measles that crossed the placenta from her mother and arrive in her milk. Her protection is natural passive immunity. Her own plasma cells made none of these antibodies, so she has no memory cells for measles, and the protection fades over the coming months.

Her cousin Petrus is 12 months old and is given a measles vaccine at the clinic. The vaccine contains measles antigens in a harmless form. His macrophages, T-helper cells and B-lymphocytes respond, and he forms plasma cells and memory cells. His immunity is artificial active immunity. It takes days to weeks to develop, but the memory cells give a fast secondary response if the virus enters his body in later years. When many children in the district are vaccinated, Hileni meets the virus less often, which is herd immunity.
