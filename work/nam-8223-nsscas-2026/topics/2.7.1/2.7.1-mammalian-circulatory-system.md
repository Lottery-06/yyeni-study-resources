## Mammalian circulatory system

### A closed double circulation

The mammalian circulatory system has three parts: the heart, which is the pump, the blood vessels, which are the tubes, and the blood, which is the fluid that is carried. It is a closed circulation because the blood stays inside the heart and vessels. It is not pumped out into open spaces around the cells, so substances reach the cells through the walls of the thinnest vessels, the capillaries, by way of tissue fluid.

The circulation is double because the blood passes through the heart twice on one complete circuit of the body. In the pulmonary circulation the right side of the heart pumps deoxygenated blood to the lungs, where it picks up oxygen, and the blood returns to the left side of the heart. In the systemic circulation the left side pumps oxygenated blood to the tissues of the body, and the blood returns to the right side. The heart itself, including its chambers and valves, is taught in topic 2.7.2.

A double circulation has two advantages. Blood loses pressure as it squeezes through the narrow capillaries of the lungs, so returning it to the heart to be pumped again lets it reach the body at a higher pressure and speed than a single pass would give. And the wall between the two sides of the heart keeps oxygenated blood on the left apart from deoxygenated blood on the right, so the tissues receive blood that is fully loaded with oxygen.

### Arteries, veins and capillaries: structure and function

Each kind of vessel is built to suit the pressure of the blood it carries and the job it does.

An artery carries blood away from the heart, and that blood is at high pressure because it has just been pumped. The wall of an artery is thick and contains collagen, elastic fibres and smooth muscle. The thickness and the collagen let the wall withstand the pressure without bursting. The elastic fibres stretch as each surge of blood arrives and recoil afterwards, which evens out the surges and keeps the blood moving smoothly instead of in jerks. The smooth muscle can contract to narrow the lumen, the space inside the vessel, or relax to widen it, and so changes how much blood reaches a tissue. The lumen is narrow, which helps to keep the pressure of the blood high.

A vein carries blood towards the heart, and by the time blood reaches a vein its pressure is low. Because the pressure is low, the wall can be thin with little elastic tissue, and the lumen is wide so that it gives little resistance to flow. Low pressure alone would not return the blood, so veins have valves. A valve closes if blood begins to flow backwards, which means that blood can move in one direction only. Many veins also run between skeletal muscles. When the muscles contract they squeeze the veins and push blood towards the heart, and the valves hold it there until the next squeeze.

A capillary joins an arteriole to a venule, and its job is exchange. Its wall is a single layer of thin endothelial cells, so substances have a very short distance to diffuse between the blood and the tissue. The lumen is only about as wide as one red blood cell, which presses the cell close to the wall and slows its passage, so there is time for oxygen to leave and carbon dioxide to enter. Capillaries form a dense network, which gives a very large surface for exchange, and there are narrow gaps between the endothelial cells that let fluid and small dissolved substances leave the blood. This is how tissue fluid is formed.

### A common mix-up: which vessel has the valves and the wide lumen

Learners sometimes swap the structures of arteries and veins, for instance by giving arteries valves or a wide lumen. The structure follows from the pressure. An artery carries blood at high pressure, so it has a thick wall and a narrow lumen. A vein carries blood at low pressure, so it has a thin wall, a wide lumen and valves. A quick test is to ask which vessel needs valves and a wide lumen: it is the one where the pressure is too low to keep blood moving forward unaided, which is the vein.

### Plan diagrams and recognising vessels on slides

A plan diagram is a drawing made at low power under the light microscope. It shows the outlines of the tissue layers, in their true proportions, and does not show individual cells. Use a sharp pencil, draw clear single lines with no shading, and give the diagram a title and the magnification. The labels sit outside the drawing, joined to it by ruled lines that do not cross. The aim is to record the arrangement of the layers, not to copy every detail.

In a transverse section an artery and a vein have the same three layers: a lining of endothelium, a middle layer of smooth muscle and elastic fibres, and an outer layer of fibrous connective tissue. What differs is how much of each is present. An artery has a thick wall, a thick middle layer and a narrow, roughly round lumen. A vein has a thinner wall, a thinner middle layer and a wider lumen that is often irregular or flattened because the wall is weak enough to collapse when the slide is made.

The ratio of lumen width to wall thickness is therefore a quick guide. A small ratio suggests an artery and a large ratio suggests a vein. Valves are not usually visible in a transverse section, so do not depend on them. A photomicrograph also needs a scale before the size of anything can be stated, so judge the vessel by the proportions of its layers. If the artery and the vein lie side by side in one view, the comparison is easy because the same magnification applies to both.

### Red blood cells and three white blood cells

A red blood cell is a biconcave disc about 7 to 8 micrometres across. It has no nucleus and is filled with haemoglobin. The biconcave shape gives a large surface area for the volume and puts the haemoglobin close to the surface, so oxygen has a short distance to diffuse. In a stained blood film the cells look like small pink or red discs with a paler centre, and they are the most numerous cells in view.

Three white blood cells are told apart by the shape of the nucleus and the amount of cytoplasm. A neutrophil is about 10 to 12 micrometres across. It has one nucleus that is divided into two to five lobes joined by thin strands, and its cytoplasm contains fine granules. A monocyte is the largest of the three, about 15 to 20 micrometres across, with one large kidney-shaped or bean-shaped nucleus and a fairly large amount of pale cytoplasm. A lymphocyte is smaller, about 7 to 10 micrometres across, with one large round nucleus that fills most of the cell and leaves a thin rim of cytoplasm.

To draw a cell from a slide or micrograph, draw the outline, then the shape and position of the nucleus and any granules, and add labels. The red blood cell in the same view is a convenient ruler: it is about 7 micrometres across, so a white cell that is clearly larger than it can be judged against it. Under the light microscope the nucleus takes up the stain and the cytoplasm is paler. An electron micrograph is in shades of grey and shows more internal detail, such as the outline of the nuclear envelope.

### A neutrophil has one nucleus, not three

A neutrophil is often described as having three nuclei. In fact it has one nucleus with several lobes. The lobes are joined by thin strands, which are hard to see or fall outside the plane of the section, so the lobes look separate. Draw one nucleus made of connected lobes. A quick test is to ask how many nuclei the cell has: the answer is one, and the lobes are parts of it.

### Tissue fluid: functions and formation

Tissue fluid is the fluid that fills the spaces between the cells of a tissue. It is the medium through which the blood and the cells exchange substances, because the cells are not in direct contact with the blood in a closed circulation. Tissue fluid brings oxygen, glucose, amino acids, fatty acids, ions and hormones to the cells, and it takes away carbon dioxide, urea and other waste products.

It forms in the capillary network in a sequence. At the arteriole end of a capillary the hydrostatic pressure of the blood is high, because the blood has just come from the arteries. This pressure forces water and small dissolved substances out through the gaps between the endothelial cells, a process called ultrafiltration. Red blood cells, platelets and large plasma proteins are too big to pass through the gaps, so they stay in the capillary. The fluid that has left is tissue fluid, and it surrounds the cells.

As the blood moves along the capillary towards the venule end its hydrostatic pressure falls, because fluid has left and the capillary is narrow. The plasma proteins that stayed behind lower the water potential of the blood, so the water potential of the blood is now lower than that of the tissue fluid. Water therefore moves back into the capillary by osmosis, down the water potential gradient, carrying some dissolved waste with it. About 90% of the fluid that left returns this way. The rest, which does not return, drains into lymph capillaries and becomes lymph.

### Blood, tissue fluid and lymph compared

Tissue fluid is formed from blood plasma that has left the capillaries. Lymph is formed from the tissue fluid that does not return to the capillaries: it drains into blind-ended lymph capillaries, and the fluid inside the lymph vessels is called lymph. The three fluids share a base of water and dissolved substances, but they differ in what they contain and where they are found.

Blood contains red blood cells, white blood cells, platelets and plasma proteins. Tissue fluid contains no red blood cells or platelets and little or no large protein, because these are too large to leave the capillary, and it has fewer white cells than blood, mostly phagocytes. Lymph is similar to tissue fluid but contains more lymphocytes, and lymph from the small intestine also carries absorbed fats. Compared with blood plasma, tissue fluid has less oxygen and glucose and more carbon dioxide, because the cells take up the first two and make the third.

The fluids also differ in position and movement. Blood is held inside blood vessels and is driven round by the heart at a higher pressure. Tissue fluid lies between cells and is not in a vessel. Lymph flows slowly at low pressure through lymph vessels that have valves, pushed along mainly by the squeezing of nearby muscles, and it drains into veins close to the heart. In this way the fluid that escaped from the blood is returned to it.

### Red blood cells carry oxygen and carbon dioxide

Haemoglobin is a protein made of four polypeptide chains. Each chain has a haem group containing one iron(II) ion, and each haem group can bind one oxygen molecule, so one haemoglobin molecule can carry up to four oxygen molecules. The reaction is reversible: Hb + 4O₂ ⇌ HbO₈, where HbO₈ is oxyhaemoglobin. Where the partial pressure of oxygen is high, as in the lungs, the reaction goes to the right and haemoglobin loads oxygen. Where it is low, as in respiring tissues, oxyhaemoglobin releases oxygen.

Carbon dioxide is carried in the opposite direction, and red blood cells deal with much of it. Carbon dioxide made by respiring cells diffuses into the blood and into red blood cells. Inside the red blood cell the enzyme carbonic anhydrase catalyses the reaction of carbon dioxide with water: CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃. The carbonic acid then dissociates: H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻. Without the enzyme the reaction is too slow to take up carbon dioxide in the short time that blood spends in a capillary.

The hydrogen ions bind to haemoglobin to form haemoglobinic acid, written HHb. Because haemoglobin takes up the hydrogen ions, the pH inside the cell changes little, so haemoglobin is acting as a buffer. The binding of hydrogen ions also helps haemoglobin to release its oxygen, which is explained under the Bohr shift. The hydrogencarbonate ions leave the red blood cell and enter the plasma.

A smaller share of the carbon dioxide binds straight to the amino groups at the ends of the polypeptide chains of haemoglobin, forming carbaminohaemoglobin. In the lungs, where the partial pressure of carbon dioxide is low, these reactions run in reverse. Carbon dioxide is released and diffuses out of the blood.

### Carbon monoxide and haemoglobin

Carbon monoxide is a gas that forms when fuels such as wood, charcoal or tobacco burn with too little oxygen. Haemoglobin has a much higher affinity for carbon monoxide than for oxygen, more than 200 times its affinity for oxygen. Carbon monoxide binds to the iron of a haem group and forms carboxyhaemoglobin, which releases carbon monoxide very slowly, so the combination is stable and oxygen is outcompeted.

The syllabus describes this as carbon monoxide reducing the affinity of haemoglobin for oxygen. The result is that less haemoglobin is available to carry oxygen, so haemoglobin takes up less oxygen in the lungs and the blood carries less oxygen even when breathing is normal. A person sleeping in a closed room with a charcoal fire can be exposed, and the tissues may be short of oxygen although the lungs are working. The more of the haemoglobin that has become carboxyhaemoglobin, the smaller the amount of oxygen the blood can carry.

### The role of plasma in carrying carbon dioxide

Plasma is mostly water, so it dissolves ions and gases and carries them. It is the main route by which carbon dioxide travels, in three forms. About 85% travels as hydrogencarbonate ions, which are made inside red blood cells by carbonic anhydrase and then leave the cell and dissolve in the plasma. Roughly 5% travels as dissolved carbon dioxide molecules in the plasma. Roughly 10% is carried inside the red blood cells as carbaminohaemoglobin and is not carried in the plasma. These percentages are approximate and vary between sources, but they show that plasma carries most of the carbon dioxide.

A common confusion is between hydrogencarbonate ions and carbon dioxide bound to haemoglobin. In fact they are different forms. The hydrogencarbonate ion is an ion dissolved in the plasma; carbaminohaemoglobin is a carbon dioxide molecule attached to a protein inside a red blood cell. A quick test is to ask which form carries most of the carbon dioxide: it is the hydrogencarbonate ion in the plasma.

In the lungs the sequence reverses. The partial pressure of carbon dioxide in the alveoli is low, so hydrogencarbonate ions move from the plasma back into the red blood cells. There they combine with hydrogen ions to form carbonic acid, and carbonic anhydrase converts this into carbon dioxide and water. The carbon dioxide diffuses out of the cell, through the plasma and into the alveoli, down the gradient of partial pressure.

### The oxygen dissociation curve of adult haemoglobin

The partial pressure of oxygen, written pO₂, is the part of the total gas pressure that is due to oxygen. It is measured in kilopascals (kPa). An oxygen dissociation curve plots the percentage saturation of haemoglobin with oxygen on the vertical axis against pO₂ on the horizontal axis. It shows how readily haemoglobin picks up and releases oxygen.

For adult haemoglobin at normal blood pH the curve is S-shaped. Using approximate values, haemoglobin is about 50% saturated at 3.5 kPa, about 75% at 5.3 kPa, about 90% at 8 kPa and about 97% at 13 kPa. The curve is shallow at low pO₂, steep in the middle and flat near 100%.

The S-shape has a cause. Each haemoglobin molecule is folded, and the haem groups are partly buried inside it, so at a low pO₂ the first oxygen molecule binds with difficulty and the curve is shallow. Once it has bound, the shape of the molecule changes, which makes it easier for the second and third oxygen molecules to bind, and the curve becomes steep. Near saturation few unoccupied haem groups remain, so it is harder for each further oxygen molecule to find a place, and the curve flattens.

The word affinity needs care. Haemoglobin has an affinity for oxygen, which means that it binds oxygen readily or less readily. It is wrong to say that oxygen has an affinity for haemoglobin. A curve further to the left shows a higher affinity of haemoglobin for oxygen, because the haemoglobin is more saturated at the same pO₂. A curve further to the right shows a lower affinity.

### Why the shape of the curve matters in the lungs and the tissues

In the alveoli of the lungs the pO₂ is about 13 kPa. This is on the flat part of the curve, where haemoglobin is about 97% saturated. Because the curve is flat there, a fall in pO₂ of a few kPa reduces the saturation only slightly, so blood still leaves the lungs well loaded. This gives a margin of safety, for instance when the air is thinner at altitude.

In respiring tissues the pO₂ is lower, about 5 kPa in resting muscle, and it falls lower in active muscle. That is on the steep part of the curve, where a small fall in pO₂ causes a large fall in saturation. Oxyhaemoglobin therefore releases a large amount of oxygen to the cells for a small change in pO₂.

The pO₂ falls most in the tissues that respire fastest, so haemoglobin releases most oxygen where the need is greatest. From the approximate values above, blood that leaves the lungs about 97% saturated and leaves a resting tissue about 75% saturated has released 97 − 75 = 22 percentage points of its oxygen. It releases more when the tissue is active.

### The Bohr shift

The Bohr shift is the shift of the oxygen dissociation curve to the right when the partial pressure of carbon dioxide rises and the pH falls. At a given pO₂ the haemoglobin is then less saturated, which means that its affinity for oxygen is lower.

The mechanism has a chain of steps. Carbon dioxide dissolves in the blood and forms carbonic acid, which dissociates and releases hydrogen ions. pH measures hydrogen ion concentration on a scale that runs the opposite way, so a lower pH means a higher hydrogen ion concentration, not a lower one. The hydrogen ions bind to haemoglobin, as haemoglobinic acid. This changes its shape and lowers its affinity for oxygen, so oxygen is released.

The shift is useful because the carbon dioxide comes from respiration. Respiring tissues release carbon dioxide, so in a tissue that respires quickly the curve shifts further to the right. Haemoglobin then unloads more oxygen at the same pO₂, and the cells that need most oxygen receive most. In the lungs carbon dioxide leaves the blood, the pH rises and the curve shifts back to the left, so haemoglobin loads oxygen readily.

It helps to keep two terms apart. Respiration is the set of reactions in cells that release energy from glucose, using oxygen and producing carbon dioxide and water; it does not produce oxygen. Breathing is the movement of air into and out of the lungs. The Bohr shift depends on the carbon dioxide made by respiration in cells and not on the movement of air. A quick test is to ask whether something is a reaction in cells or the movement of gas at the lungs.

### The red blood cell count at high altitude

A hiker from Windhoek who spends several weeks at a camp on a high mountain provides an example. As altitude increases the air pressure falls. Oxygen is still about 21% of the air, but its partial pressure falls with the total pressure. Less oxygen diffuses into the blood in the lungs, so the haemoglobin leaving the lungs is less saturated.

The kidneys respond to the low oxygen content of the blood by releasing the hormone erythropoietin. It stimulates the bone marrow to make more red blood cells, and the red blood cell count rises over a period of days to weeks. Suppose the count rises from 5.0 million per mm³ to 6.3 million per mm³. That is an increase of 6.3 − 5.0 = 1.3 million per mm³.

The significance is that a higher count means more haemoglobin in each litre of blood. The blood therefore carries more oxygen even though each haemoglobin molecule is less saturated, and delivery to the tissues comes closer to what it was at low altitude. The extra cells also tend to make the blood thicker, which can increase the work of the heart. The rise is gradual, so a person who climbs fast has not yet gained the benefit.
