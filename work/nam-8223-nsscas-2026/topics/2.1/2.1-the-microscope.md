## The microscope

### The light microscope

A light microscope forms its image with visible light. Light from a lamp passes through a thin, partly transparent specimen and is bent by glass lenses, first the objective lens and then the eyepiece lens, to build up an enlarged image that the eye or a camera records. Cells, tissues and organelles such as the nucleus and the chloroplasts can be seen. The specimen has to be thin enough for light to pass through, but that is all, so living cells can be examined, and stains are often added to make parts easier to tell apart, so the image can be in colour.

The detail a light microscope can show is limited. Visible light has a wavelength of roughly 400 to 700 nm, and two points closer together than about 0.2 µm (200 nm) blur into one. That is the resolution of a good light microscope, and a useful magnification stops at about ×1500. Mitochondria appear as small specks, but the folded membranes inside them are far below this limit.

### The electron microscope

An electron microscope forms its image with a beam of electrons instead of light. The beam is produced by an electron gun and focused by electromagnets, which do the work that glass lenses do in a light microscope. Air scatters electrons, so the whole path from the gun to the specimen is a vacuum. The image cannot be seen directly: it appears on a fluorescent screen or is recorded electronically, and it is black and white, in shades of grey.

The great advantage is resolution. An electron beam behaves as a wave with a wavelength many thousands of times shorter than that of visible light, so points very close together still give separate images. A transmission electron microscope resolves detail of the order of 1 nm, and useful magnifications run to several hundred thousand times. The price is the preparation. The specimen sits in a vacuum, is dehydrated and fixed, and is treated with heavy metal compounds that scatter electrons. Living material therefore cannot be examined, and some structures seen may be artefacts of the preparation rather than features of the living cell.

### Transmission and scanning electron microscopes

In a transmission electron microscope (TEM) the beam passes through a very thin section of the specimen. Dense parts, especially those stained with heavy metals, scatter more electrons and look darker, so the image shows the internal structure of the cell in two dimensions: the nucleus, mitochondria, membranes and ribosomes appear in section.

In a scanning electron microscope (SEM) the beam is moved across the surface of a specimen that has been coated with a thin layer of metal such as gold. Electrons knocked from or reflected by the surface are collected to build up the image. The result is a three-dimensional view of the outside of the specimen, with depth and shadow, such as the surface of a pollen grain or an insect eye. A scanning electron microscope has a lower resolution than a transmission electron microscope, of the order of several nanometres, but it can examine thick specimens because the beam does not have to pass through them.

### A scanning electron micrograph shows a surface

A common error is to say that a scanning electron micrograph shows the inside of a cell, or a thin slice of it. It does not. It shows the outer surface in three dimensions, because the image is built from electrons coming off the surface. A transmission electron micrograph shows the interior of a thin section, flat. The test is to ask whether the micrograph shows a surface or an interior. A picture with apparent depth, highlights and shadows, and no cut edges, is a surface. A flat picture of membranes and organelles in section is an interior.

### Resolution and magnification

Magnification is how many times larger the image is than the real object. A drawing 10 times as long as the specimen has a magnification of ×10. Resolution is the smallest distance at which two points can still be seen as separate. It is a property of the microscope, set mainly by the wavelength of the radiation used: the shorter the wavelength, the smaller the distance between two points that can still be told apart. A light microscope resolves about 0.2 µm (200 nm). An electron microscope resolves about 1 nm in practice, roughly 200 times finer, so it can show structures that a light microscope blurs together.

The two ideas are different. Magnification says how big the image is; resolution says how much detail it holds. Zoom a phone photograph of a distant hillside until it fills the screen and it becomes a larger blur, with no more detail than before. A microscope with poor resolution behaves the same way: two cell structures 0.1 µm apart merge in a light microscope at ×400 and at ×1500 alike, because the microscope cannot separate them. That is why electron microscopes are valued: they give both high magnification and high resolution.

### Bigger is not clearer

A common error is to say that an image has a high resolution because it is greatly magnified, or to treat the two words as the same. Suppose a learner enlarges a photomicrograph taken at ×400 on a photocopier until it is ×1600. The image is 1600 ÷ 400 = 4 times bigger, yet two structures that were blurred together are still blurred. No more detail has been revealed, so the resolution has not improved. Magnification beyond what the resolution supports adds size but no detail, and this is called empty magnification. The test is to ask whether making the image bigger shows any more detail. If it does not, magnification alone has changed. Resolution also belongs to the microscope, not to the picture, so say that the microscope has a high resolution, not the image.

### Units of length

Lengths in microscopy are measured in millimetres (mm), micrometres (µm) and nanometres (nm). Each is one thousand times smaller than the one before: 1 mm = 1000 µm, 1 µm = 1000 nm, and so 1 mm = 1 000 000 nm.

To move to the next smaller unit, multiply by 1000: 0.06 mm × 1000 = 60 µm. To move to the next larger unit, divide by 1000: 1500 nm ÷ 1000 = 1.5 µm. Most cells are best described in micrometres. A human cheek cell is about 60 µm across and a bacterium about 1 to 2 µm. A virus is measured in nanometres. Mixed units are a frequent source of wrong answers in this topic, so convert both lengths to one unit before dividing or multiplying.

### Calibrating the eyepiece graticule

An eyepiece graticule is a small glass disc with a ruler etched on it, placed in the eyepiece so that it is seen in focus on top of the specimen. Its divisions have no fixed size: the length each one stands for depends on the objective lens in use. The graticule must therefore be calibrated for each objective. A stage micrometer is a slide with a scale of known length etched on it, often 1 mm divided into 100 parts of 0.01 mm each.

First, place the stage micrometer on the stage and bring its scale into focus with the chosen objective. Then turn the eyepiece until the graticule scale lies alongside the stage micrometer scale, and line up the zero marks. Next, read how many graticule divisions match a known length on the stage micrometer, using a long stretch of the scale to reduce the error. Finally, divide the known length by the number of divisions.

For example, if 50 graticule divisions line up with 0.50 mm on the stage micrometer, then 0.50 ÷ 50 = 0.01 mm, so one division is 0.01 mm, which is 10 µm. Now replace the micrometer with the slide and count how many divisions a cell spans. A cell spanning 7 divisions is 7 × 10 = 70 µm long. If the objective is changed, this calibration no longer holds and must be repeated.

### Magnification, image size and actual size

Linear magnification compares two lengths: magnification = image size ÷ actual size. The image may be a drawing, a photomicrograph (a photograph taken through a light microscope) or an electron micrograph. Rearranged, actual size = image size ÷ magnification, and image size = magnification × actual size. Magnification is a ratio of two lengths, so it has no unit and is written ×600.

Two rules prevent most errors. Both lengths must be in the same unit before dividing, so convert the image size from mm to µm when the actual size is in µm. And the image size is a length measured on the picture with a ruler, across the structure along the same line as the actual length.

When a micrograph carries a scale bar, the bar itself gives the magnification without any other information. Measure the bar with a ruler, convert it to the unit written on the bar, and divide by the length the bar is labelled as. To find an actual size, give the answer with its unit, millimetres, micrometres or nanometres, chosen so that the number is easy to read.

### Total magnification of a light microscope

The total magnification of a light microscope is the magnification of the eyepiece lens multiplied by that of the objective lens. With a ×10 eyepiece and a ×40 objective the total is 10 × 40 = 400, written ×400. A common error is to quote just the eyepiece or just the objective magnification as if it were the total. Both lenses enlarge the image, one after the other, so their magnifications multiply. Adding them (10 + 40 = 50) is also wrong.

### Worked examples

Example 1, actual size from a photomicrograph. A photomicrograph of a stained cheek cell is at ×600 and the cell is 36 mm across on the print. The equation is actual size = image size ÷ magnification. Substituting gives 36 ÷ 600 = 0.06 mm. Converting to micrometres, 0.06 × 1000 = 60 µm. The cell is 60 µm across.

Example 2, magnification of a drawing. A learner draws a palisade cell from a section of mopane leaf. The drawing is 48 mm long and the graticule gives the real length as 80 µm. Convert the drawing length first: 48 × 1000 = 48 000 µm. Then magnification = 48 000 ÷ 80 = 600. The drawing is ×600.

Example 3, magnification from a scale bar. An electron micrograph has a scale bar 20 mm long, labelled 2 µm. Converting, 20 × 1000 = 20 000 µm. Then magnification = 20 000 ÷ 2 = 10 000. The micrograph is ×10 000. The same micrograph then gives the actual size of any structure on it: a structure 15 mm long on the print is 15 ÷ 10 000 = 0.0015 mm, and 0.0015 × 1000 = 1.5 µm.
