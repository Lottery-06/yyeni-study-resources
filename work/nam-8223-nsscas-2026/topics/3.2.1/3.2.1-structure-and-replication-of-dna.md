## Structure and replication of DNA

### The nucleotide

A nucleotide is the building block of the nucleic acids, DNA and RNA. It is made of three parts joined together. The first is a phosphate group. The second is a pentose sugar, a sugar whose molecule holds five carbon atoms. The third is a nitrogenous base, a ring-shaped molecule that contains nitrogen and carries the genetic information.

The sugar sits at the centre of the nucleotide, with the phosphate group on one side of it and the base on the other. In a DNA nucleotide the sugar is deoxyribose. In an RNA nucleotide it is ribose. Deoxyribose has one oxygen atom fewer than ribose.

A single nucleotide is a small molecule. Many nucleotides join into a long chain to make a nucleic acid. Some nucleotides also work on their own in the cell, and the best known is ATP.

### ATP, a phosphorylated nucleotide

ATP stands for adenosine triphosphate. Its base is adenine and its sugar is ribose, so it is built on the same plan as the nucleotides of RNA. What marks it out is the chain of three phosphate groups attached to the sugar. An ordinary nucleotide such as adenosine monophosphate carries one phosphate group. ATP is therefore called a phosphorylated nucleotide, meaning a nucleotide to which extra phosphate groups have been added.

The extra phosphate groups matter because of what happens to the last one. When the end phosphate group is removed by hydrolysis, which uses a molecule of water, ATP becomes ADP (adenosine diphosphate) and a free phosphate group. This releases energy that the cell can use for processes that need it, such as moving molecules or building new ones. The cell can add a phosphate group to ADP to make ATP again, so the same molecules are used repeatedly.

### Purines and pyrimidines

DNA and RNA use five nitrogenous bases between them. They fall into two sets according to the shape of the base. Adenine and guanine are purines. A purine has a double ring structure, which means two rings joined together. Cytosine, thymine and uracil are pyrimidines. A pyrimidine has a single ring structure, so it is the smaller of the two molecules.

DNA contains adenine, guanine, cytosine and thymine. RNA contains adenine, guanine, cytosine and uracil, so uracil takes the place of thymine in RNA. Each base sits on the sugar of its nucleotide, and which base a nucleotide carries is what distinguishes one nucleotide of a nucleic acid from another.

### The structure of DNA and RNA

A DNA molecule is made of two strands, each a polynucleotide, which is a long chain of nucleotides. Within a strand, the sugar of one nucleotide is joined to the phosphate group of the next by a strong covalent bond. The joining releases a molecule of water, so it is a condensation reaction. The repeating sugars and phosphate groups form the sugar-phosphate backbone of the strand, and the bases project sideways from it.

The two strands lie side by side with their bases facing inwards. They run in opposite directions, so the start of one strand lies beside the end of the other. Each base is held to a base on the other strand by hydrogen bonds, and the whole molecule is twisted into a double helix. The sugar in DNA is deoxyribose, and the bases are adenine, thymine, guanine and cytosine.

An RNA molecule is usually a single polynucleotide strand. Its sugar is ribose and its bases include uracil instead of thymine. It is much shorter than the DNA it is copied from. Some RNA strands fold back on themselves so that parts of the strand pair up; this is described with transfer RNA in topic 3.2.2, Protein synthesis. In summary, DNA is double-stranded with deoxyribose and thymine, whereas RNA is usually single-stranded with ribose and uracil.

### Base pairing and hydrogen bonds

The bases on the two strands of DNA do not pair at random. A purine always lies opposite a pyrimidine, and the pairing is specific: adenine pairs with thymine, and cytosine pairs with guanine. This is complementary base pairing. In RNA, adenine pairs with uracil.

The pairs are held together by hydrogen bonds, and the number differs. Adenine and thymine are joined by two hydrogen bonds. Cytosine and guanine are joined by three. Because every pair contains one double-ring purine and one single-ring pyrimidine, each pair has the same width, so the helix keeps an even width along its length.

Base pairing is important for three reasons. First, the base sequence of one strand fixes the base sequence of the other, so either strand can act as a template when the molecule is copied, and the copy is accurate. Second, a single hydrogen bond is weak, so the strands can be separated when the DNA has to be copied or read, yet the many bonds along the molecule together hold the helix stable. Third, the number of bonds affects how easily a region comes apart. A region rich in cytosine and guanine tends to separate less easily than a region of the same length rich in adenine and thymine, because it has more hydrogen bonds for the same number of base pairs.

### Replication of DNA in S phase

Replication happens during S phase, the part of interphase in which a cell copies its DNA, before the cell divides by mitosis. The process runs in a sequence.

First, the double helix is unwound and the hydrogen bonds between the base pairs are broken. The two strands come apart at a point called a replication fork, which exposes the bases of each old strand. Each old strand now serves as a template.

Then free nucleotides from the nucleus pair with the exposed bases by complementary base pairing: adenine opposite thymine, and cytosine opposite guanine. Neighbouring nucleotides are joined together to form a new strand against each old strand. In a eukaryotic chromosome replication starts at many places along the DNA at once, which lets a very long molecule be copied in a reasonable time.

The result is two DNA molecules that are identical to the original molecule and to each other, because the base sequence of each new strand was fixed by the old strand it was built against. Each chromosome now consists of two sister chromatids joined at the centromere, and each chromatid holds one of the two new DNA molecules. The chromatids are separated later in mitosis (topic 3.1.2, Chromosome behaviour in mitosis).

This replication is semi-conservative. Each new DNA molecule holds one whole original strand and one whole newly made strand. The word "conservative" says that an old strand is kept, and "semi" says that only one of the two strands in each new molecule is old.

### Helicase and DNA polymerase

Two enzymes do most of the work of replication. The syllabus objective does not name them, but knowing what each does makes the process easier to explain and to remember.

Helicase acts first. It breaks the hydrogen bonds between the paired bases, which unwinds the helix and separates the two strands.

DNA polymerase acts next. It joins the free nucleotides that have paired with the exposed bases of a template strand, so that a new strand is built, one nucleotide after another. The free nucleotides arrive as triphosphates, like ATP, with three phosphate groups each. When a nucleotide is joined to the growing strand, two of its phosphate groups are lost and the energy released drives the joining. Helicase and polymerase are enzymes, and how enzymes work is taught in topic 2.5.1, Mode of action of enzymes.

### Molecule or strand?

A common error is to say that each new DNA molecule contains half of an old strand joined to half of a new strand, or that the old strands are split up. This confuses a DNA molecule with a strand. In semi-conservative replication, each new molecule holds one whole old strand and one whole new strand, and neither old strand is cut in half.

A quick test is to ask how many old strands are in each new molecule. The answer is one. A second test is to count the strands in a worked example. One molecule has 2 strands. After one round of replication there are 2 molecules, which hold 4 strands between them: 2 old and 2 new. After a second round there are 4 molecules, with 4 molecules × 2 strands = 8 strands, of which 2 are still the original strands and 6 are new. The 2 original strands are in 2 of the 4 molecules, one in each, and the other 2 molecules consist of new strands alone.

### Worked example: reading a base count

A sample of double-stranded DNA from a plant is found to contain 32 per cent adenine. The task is to work out the percentages of the other three bases.

The working follows from base pairing. Adenine pairs with thymine, so the sample holds as much thymine as adenine, which is 32 per cent. Adenine and thymine together make 32 + 32 = 64 per cent of the bases. The remainder is 100 − 64 = 36 per cent, and it is shared equally between guanine and cytosine because they pair with each other. 36 ÷ 2 = 18 per cent each. The purines, adenine and guanine, therefore add up to 32 + 18 = 50 per cent, and so do the pyrimidines.

In single-stranded RNA there is no second strand for the bases to pair with, so the amounts of adenine and uracil need not be equal.

### Planning a model of replication

This objective asks you to design and make a model. Making the model is practical work done by hand, so these notes cover the planning and reasoning.

Start with the materials. They have to let you join bases and then separate them again. Paper strips with card tabs, or beads on wire, work if the parts for each base fit only their partner. Show the different hydrogen bonding by joining adenine to thymine with two links and cytosine to guanine with three.

The steps then follow the process in order. First, build one DNA molecule from two strands with complementary bases and the strands running in opposite directions. Then separate the strands by unhooking the links between the base pairs, which models what helicase does. Next, build a new strand against each old strand by adding complementary bases one at a time, which models what DNA polymerase does. The result is two molecules.

To illustrate semi-conservative replication, make the original strands in one colour and the new strands in another. The colours let you tell old from new. The model is correct when each of the two final molecules has one strand of each colour and every new base matches its partner by complementary base pairing. If a molecule has two strands of the same colour, the model shows a different process.

### What a model cannot show

Every model leaves something out, and you may be asked to judge yours. A paper or bead model of replication is usually flat, so it does not show the twisting of the helix. It does not show the enzymes working. Its links do not differ in strength the way real hydrogen bonds do. It does not show that replication begins at many places along a chromosome. A good evaluation says what the model shows well, such as the order of events, and then names a limit and says why it matters.
