## The response of the immune system to pathogens

### Antigens: self and non-self

An antigen is a molecule, usually a protein or a glycoprotein, on the surface of a cell or pathogen that triggers an immune response. Antigens are not confined to pathogens. The cells of your own body carry antigens on their surface membranes, and so do bacteria, viruses, fungi, cells from another person and the cells of a transplanted organ.

Self antigens are the antigens on the body's own cells. The immune system learns early in life to leave these alone, so they do not normally trigger a response. Non-self antigens are antigens that are not part of the body: those on pathogens, on cells from another organism and on transplanted tissue. The immune system recognises a non-self antigen as foreign, and it triggers a response. The difference is therefore one of origin. Self antigens belong to the body's own cells, whereas non-self antigens do not.

Antigens are specific. An antibody, or a receptor on a lymphocyte, has a binding site whose shape is complementary to one antigen, so it binds that antigen and not others. A single bacterium carries many different antigens, so the body can respond to several of them at once. The same specificity explains why a response to one pathogen does not protect against an unrelated one.

### Phagocytosis

Phagocytes are white blood cells that destroy pathogens by phagocytosis. Phagocytosis is the engulfing of a pathogen by a cell and its digestion with enzymes. It is a chain of linked steps.

First the phagocyte binds to the pathogen. Its cell surface membrane then flows round the pathogen and encloses it in a vesicle inside the cytoplasm. Lysosomes next fuse with this vesicle and release hydrolytic enzymes into it. The enzymes break the pathogen down into small molecules. Because the pathogen is held inside a vesicle, the enzymes digest it without harming the cytoplasm of the phagocyte itself.

Phagocytosis does not depend on which antigen a pathogen carries. A phagocyte therefore acts on a wide range of pathogens, and it can begin work as soon as it reaches the infection. This is why phagocytes are the body's quick, general defence, while the lymphocyte response described below is slower and specific.

### Neutrophils and macrophages

Neutrophils and macrophages are the two phagocytes you must know. They differ in structure and in what they do after digestion.

A neutrophil has a nucleus divided into several lobes, and its cytoplasm is rich in lysosomes. It is abundant in the blood and short-lived. During a bacterial infection the number of neutrophils in the blood rises, because more are released from the bone marrow. Neutrophils leave the blood, move into the infected tissue and engulf the bacteria there. Dead neutrophils are a large part of pus.

A macrophage is larger than a neutrophil and lives for longer, in the tissues. It also engulfs and digests pathogens, using the same steps. What sets it apart is what it does next. A macrophage places antigens from the pathogen on its own cell surface membrane. This is called presenting the antigens, and it is the link between the quick general defence and the specific response.

### The primary immune response: macrophage and T-helper cells

The primary immune response is the response when the body meets a particular antigen for the first time. It is specific to that antigen, and it takes several days to produce plenty of antibody.

The sequence begins with a macrophage. It engulfs and digests a pathogen, then presents antigens from the pathogen on its own surface membrane. Only a few T-helper cells have a receptor whose shape is complementary to that antigen. One of them binds to the presented antigen and is activated. The activated T-helper cell divides by mitosis, which forms a clone of identical T-helper cells.

The clone of T-helper cells releases chemical signals, called cytokines. These signals stimulate two other kinds of lymphocyte to divide: B-lymphocytes whose receptors fit the same antigen, and T-killer cells. T-helper cells do not destroy pathogens and do not secrete antibodies. They coordinate the response.

### The primary immune response: B-lymphocytes, plasma cells and T-killer cells

A B-lymphocyte carries antibody molecules in its surface membrane, and these act as its receptors. The B-lymphocyte whose receptors fit the antigen binds it. Stimulated by the cytokines from T-helper cells, it divides by mitosis to form a clone. The cells of this clone become plasma cells and memory cells.

Plasma cells are the cells that make antibodies. They secrete large numbers of antibody molecules into the blood and tissue fluid. Each antibody is a protein with a binding site that fits the antigen, so it binds the pathogen and marks it for destruction by phagocytes. Memory cells stay in the body, and they are described in a later section.

T-killer cells that have been stimulated also divide. They destroy body cells that are infected and display the non-self antigen on their surface. Meanwhile the pathogen multiplies, because few lymphocytes match the antigen at the start and they must be selected and must divide before enough antibody exists. This delay is why a person can become ill during a first infection.

### Two errors to avoid

The first error is to say that a macrophage displays pieces of pathogen. In fact the macrophage places antigens from the pathogen on its own surface membrane. The test is to ask what exactly sits on the macrophage's membrane. The answer is the antigens, held in the membrane, where a T-helper cell can bind them.

The second error is to treat antibodies as cells made by B-lymphocytes cloning. A B-lymphocyte clones into plasma cells and memory cells. Plasma cells secrete antibodies, which are proteins. The test is to ask which cell makes antibodies and what an antibody is. The cell is a plasma cell, and an antibody is a molecule, not a cell.

### Memory cells and the secondary immune response

Memory cells are long-lived cells formed when lymphocytes clone in the primary response. They remain in the body, and they carry receptors for the antigen that stimulated them. After the first infection there are therefore many more cells that match that antigen than there were before it.

The secondary immune response is the response when the same antigen is met again. The memory cells bind the antigen and are stimulated quickly, because they are numerous and need little searching for. They divide rapidly into plasma cells. As a result antibody appears within a day or two instead of after a week or more, it reaches a much higher concentration, and it stays high for longer. The pathogen tends to be destroyed before it has multiplied enough to cause symptoms, so the person often does not feel ill.

### Long-term immunity

Long-term immunity is protection that lasts for years because memory cells persist after the first infection. The memory cells make a fast, large secondary response each time the same antigen returns. How long the protection lasts varies with the pathogen. For some infections it lasts for many years, and in some cases for life.

A memory cell recognises only the antigen that stimulated it. If a pathogen changes its antigens, as some influenza viruses do from one season to the next, the memory cells no longer match, and the body must make a new primary response. This is one reason a person can catch influenza more than once.

### Reading antibody data

A graph or table of antibody concentration against time gives the story of the response. After a first exposure there is a lag of several days with no antibody, then a slow rise to a low peak, then a fall. After a second exposure to the same antigen the lag is much shorter, the rise is steep, the peak is much higher and the concentration stays high for longer.

The lag in the first response is the time taken to select and clone the matching lymphocytes. A count of plasma cells reaches its peak before the antibody concentration does, because antibody is made by the plasma cells and builds up in the blood. When you describe data, quote values with their units, then say what the pattern means.

### Worked example: a cut on the hand

Ndapanda cuts her hand on a thorn while working in a garden in Ondangwa, and bacteria enter the wound. Neutrophils move from the blood into the tissue and engulf the bacteria, so the number of neutrophils in her blood rises. Macrophages in the tissue engulf more bacteria and present their antigens on their own surface membranes. A T-helper cell with a matching receptor binds, divides and releases cytokines. A B-lymphocyte with a matching receptor is stimulated, clones, and forms plasma cells and memory cells. The plasma cells secrete antibodies, and T-killer cells destroy any of her body cells that have been infected.

Six months later the same bacterium enters another cut. Memory cells from the first infection bind the antigen at once and divide rapidly into plasma cells. Antibody appears sooner and at a higher concentration than before, so the bacteria are destroyed before they cause the swelling of the first infection.
