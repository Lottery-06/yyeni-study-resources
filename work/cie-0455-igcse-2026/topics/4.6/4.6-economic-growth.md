## What economic growth means

### The definition and its boundary

Economic growth is the sustained increase in an economy's real output of goods and services over time, measured by the rise in real Gross Domestic Product. Each word carries weight. Sustained means the rise runs across years, not a single quarter. Real means the rise is stripped of price changes, so output that looks bigger because prices rose does not count. Output means what the economy produces: goods such as steel and maize alongside services such as banking and tourism. The boundary matters too: a return to a previous peak after a downturn is a recovery, and growth means going beyond what the economy produced before.

### Two economies over a decade

Take two economies over a decade. In one, lodges round Etosha, farms and workshops each add a little output in most years, and the region's real GDP climbs from N$1 000 million to N$1 200 million over ten years; that sustained climb is growth. In the other, a single mine opens, output jumps for two years and slips back; that one-off rise is not growth, because growth asks what happened over years, not what one lucky year did. Vietnam's rise from a mostly farming economy to an industrial one shows the same idea outside southern Africa: it was the decade of climbing output, not any single year, that growth names.

### Growth is not development

Economic growth is about output; economic development, taught in topic 5.1, is about how life changes for people - health, education, what people can afford. Growth tends to help development along, since a bigger output gives more to spend on schools and clinics, yet the two are told apart by what they measure, and one can move without the other.

## Measuring growth: real GDP and GDP per head

### Real GDP and nominal GDP

Real Gross Domestic Product is the value of all the final goods and services an economy produces in a year, measured at constant prices - the prices of a base year - so that price rises alone cannot make output seem larger. Nominal GDP is the same yearly production valued at current prices. The two part company whenever prices move: an economy whose prices doubled while its production stood still would show a nominal GDP twice as large and a real GDP unchanged, so real GDP is the measure of growth.

### The growth-rate formula, worked

Growth is measured as a percentage rate. The formula: growth rate = (real GDP in the later year - real GDP in the earlier year) / real GDP in the earlier year x 100. Work it with invented figures. An economy produces N$50 000 million of real GDP in 2025 and N$52 500 million in 2026. The rise is N$52 500 million - N$50 000 million = N$2 500 million. Divided by the earlier year, N$2 500 million / N$50 000 million = 0.05, and 0.05 x 100 = 5, so the economy grew by 5% that year.

### GDP per head

Total real GDP can mislead when populations differ. GDP per head is real GDP divided by the population, and it states the average output per person, the figure used to compare average living standards between economies or across years. Where population grows as fast as output, total real GDP rises while GDP per head stands still, and new output is absorbed by new people. GDP per head is an average, so it may hide how output is shared: a rising average can sit beside households whose incomes did not move.

## Recession: meaning, causes and consequences

### What a recession is

A recession is a period in which real GDP falls for two successive quarters or longer, so total output is contracting rather than growing. A fall in one quarter alone does not yet meet the definition; the test is duration as well as direction.

### How a recession begins

A recession often begins on the demand side: households cut spending as incomes or confidence fall, firms delay investment as sales weaken, and export orders drop as customers abroad buy less. The chain runs: total demand falls, so firms cut production and lay off workers, so incomes fall further and demand falls again. A second route runs through finance: where a banking crisis makes loans scarce and costly, firms that depend on credit cut back fast, as export-led economies such as Germany's do when foreign orders fall away.

### Inside the production possibility curve

On a production possibility curve, a recession is a movement inward, not a shift. In words: two goods on the axes, the curve showing what the economy produces at full use of resources, and the economy first at a point on the curve. As output falls, the economy slides to a point inside the curve: the boundary has not moved, but the economy now produces below what it could, and the gap between curve and point is output the recession wastes - idle workers and machines.

### A consequence is not a cause

The confusion: asked why a recession happened, learners offer what it caused - rising unemployment, firms' losses - as though these brought it about. The boundary: causes come before the downturn and produce it; consequences follow from it. The test: ask whether the item itself needs the recession to have happened first.

### Who a recession hits

The consequences land unevenly: workers lose jobs and incomes; firms lose sales and some fail; the government's tax revenue falls as benefit spending rises, squeezing its budget from both ends; consumers meet a narrower choice as businesses cut back.

## The causes of economic growth

### Two routes to higher output

Output rises in two ways, and the causes differ. Where the economy sits inside its production possibility curve, with workers idle and machines standing unused, a rise in total demand - more spending by households, firms, the government or buyers abroad - puts the idle resources to work, and the economy moves toward the curve: real GDP rises and the utilisation of resources rises with it, though capacity is unchanged. Where the economy already produces at the curve, output grows where capacity grows: investment in new capital, better technology, and more or better-trained workers shift the whole curve outward, and with it the maximum the economy can produce.

### The movement toward the curve

Trace the demand route. Starting point: total spending rises in an economy with spare capacity. Mechanism: because firms see more customers, they raise production, take on idle workers and run machines longer; this causes real GDP to climb as unemployed resources are put to use. Consequence: the economy moves from its point inside the curve to a point on it, and output reaches what its existing capacity allows. The limit: once at the boundary, further spending has no idle resources to employ and tends to raise prices instead.

### The outward shift, worked

Trace the capacity route with a firm. Marula Foods opens a second cannery in Gobabis and trains its staff on new equipment: the town can now process more fruit with the same number of pickers, and its maximum output has risen. What one firm does, investment does for the economy: new machines, better technology and trained workers raise the most the economy can produce, so the curve itself shifts outward. Vietnam's industrial expansion carried the same logic at national scale: year after year of new factories and rising skills moved the country's boundary outward.

## The consequences of economic growth

### What growth tends to bring

Where its gains are shared out, growth tends to raise household incomes, create jobs and lift the government's tax revenue, easing the financing of schools, clinics and roads. Average living standards tend to improve as output per person rises, provided the gains are distributed across households rather than held by a few. An economy with idle workers gains quickly from growth that employs them, since output rises without capacity having to be built first.

### The costs growth may carry

Growth may carry costs. Where demand presses on capacity, prices rise rather than output. Expansion may impose costs on others: smoke, congestion and depleted resources are side effects that the people who enjoy the output do not pay for. Devoting resources to capital goods has an opportunity cost - consumer goods given up now so output can rise later - and where the gains from growth flow to a narrow group, inequality may widen even as output climbs.

### Different economies, different balances

The balance of costs and benefits is not the same everywhere. A lower-income economy with idle resources may weigh the income gains of growth heavily and accept costs it cannot yet afford to prevent; a higher-income economy producing near capacity may find that extra demand brings mostly price rises, and may weigh the environmental costs more heavily. Brazil's fast-growing cities have shown both faces at once - incomes rising while congestion and air quality worsen - which is why the consequences of growth are weighed in the context of the economy that grows.

## Policies to promote economic growth

### The policy set

The policies that promote growth are macroeconomic policies: they act on the whole economy, not on one market or firm. On the demand side sit the fiscal measures of topic 4.3 - government spending and taxation - and the interest rate of topic 4.4. On the capacity side sit supply-side measures: education and training, infrastructure such as roads and ports, and incentives such as lower direct taxes. The Netherlands shows the capacity route outside southern Africa, with decades spent on flood defences that raised what its economy could produce.

### Demand-side, traced

Where the economy has spare capacity, a demand-side policy can raise output quickly. Trace a cut in the interest rate: borrowing becomes cheaper, so households bring forward purchases and firms fund investment; total spending rises, and firms meet it by hiring idle workers, so real GDP climbs as the economy moves toward its production possibility curve. At the curve already, the same policy finds no idle resources, and the extra spending tends to raise prices instead.

### Supply-side, traced

Capacity measures work more slowly and last longer. Trace a training programme: workers who produce more per hour let firms fill larger orders without larger workforces, so the economy's maximum output rises and the curve shifts outward. A new road to a port does the same: transport costs fall, exports reach distant markets at competitive prices, and firms expand production to meet the sales.

### Which policy, when

Effectiveness depends on where the economy stands. With idle resources, demand-side measures can lift output within a year or two; producing at the boundary, they mostly lift prices, and growth needs capacity measures instead. Capacity measures take years to arrive and need sustained finance, which a government with a thin tax base may struggle to hold. The choice turns on the economy's position, the time horizon it faces and the quality of its delivery.

### Macroeconomic is not microeconomic

The confusion: learners list measures acting on one market or firm as though they acted on the whole economy. The boundary: a macroeconomic policy works on economy-wide totals, while a microeconomic policy works on a single market or industry, such as a subsidy to one bus company. The test: ask what the measure reaches - the whole economy, or one part of it.
