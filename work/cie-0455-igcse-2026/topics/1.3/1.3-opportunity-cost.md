# 1.3 Opportunity cost

*cie-0455-igcse-2026. Offline study notes, rendered from the approved content units on 2026-10-06. Everything here is also what the flashcards are built from.*

**What this topic asks of you**

- Define opportunity cost precisely, and separate it from the money cost of a choice and from the list of everything given up.
- Analyse how opportunity cost influences the decisions of consumers, workers, producers and governments: what is weighed against what, and when a choice is worthwhile.

---

## The cost of the road not taken

### What opportunity cost means

Every choice closes a door. When a farmer in the Otavi area plants one field with maize, the same field cannot carry the sunflower crop it might have carried instead. Opportunity cost is the benefit lost from the next best alternative that is given up when a choice is made: the maize is chosen, and the harvest the sunflowers would have yielded is the cost. Two rules make the definition precise. First, the cost is the next best alternative, not all the alternatives forgone stacked together - the farmer considered sunflowers and Lucerne hay, but once sunflowers ranked second, hay drops out of the reckoning. Second, the opportunity cost is not the money spent. If the maize seed cost N$2 000, that is the money cost; the opportunity cost is the sunflower harvest forgone, which money may not measure at all.

### Why the cost exists at all

Opportunity cost exists because resources are scarce. A field, a worker's morning, a government's budget rand: each can serve one use at a time, so using it one way means not using it another. If resources were abundant enough for every purpose, choosing would sacrifice nothing and opportunity cost would vanish. This is why the idea sits at the centre of the subject: it is the economic problem of topic 1.1 translated into a number - the measured benefit of what the choice gave up. A student in Japan choosing between two hours of extra mathematics and two hours at a part-time job that pays 2 400 yen faces the same structure: the time is scarce, and the pay forgone is the cost of the study.

### Connections

Opportunity cost is the working form of the economic problem from topic 1.1, and topic 1.4 draws it as the production possibility curve, where moving along the boundary shows the output of one good given up for another.

## Weighing alternatives: cost in real decisions

### The weighing that decides

A decision maker does not ask what a choice costs in money alone; the question is what the same effort would have earned elsewhere. The method: list the realistic alternatives, rank them by expected benefit, choose the best - and the opportunity cost is the expected benefit of the runner-up. A choice tends to be worthwhile when the benefit of what is chosen exceeds the benefit of that runner-up. Note what this is not: the decision to choose is the act of picking; the opportunity cost is the measured sacrifice that act creates. The two work together - the cost is what the decision is weighed against.

### The chain from cost to behaviour

Because the runner-up benefit is the benchmark, a change in it changes the choice. Starting point: a worker at Oshakati Textiles is offered a Saturday shift paying N$420, and her next best use of the Saturday is mending stock for her market stall, worth N$300 to her. Mechanism: the shift pays more than the forgone alternative, so she takes it, because the N$300 is what the Saturday really costs her. If stall trade improves and the Saturday of mending rises to N$500 in value, the same shift now costs more than it pays, and the scales tip the other way. Consequence: she declines the shift, the factory loses a willing worker, and her stall keeps the sales. The cost did not change the pay; it changed the comparison, and the comparison decides.

### Four decision makers, one logic

A consumer in Windhoek with N$120 spends it on data and gives up the taxi rides the same N$120 would have bought. Kaoko Juice runs its one bottling line on mango nectar and gives up the marula batch worth N$18 000 the line would have filled. The government of a town near Gobabis spends its N$1.5 million road fund on the tarred link to the main road and gives up the clinic upgrade the same money would have funded. In Germany, a city council weighing a new tram line against extra school places runs the identical comparison. Each decision maker weighs benefit against forgone benefit - that is the influence of opportunity cost on decision making.

### Connections

Workers weighing pay against other uses of their time reappear in topic 3.3, and governments weighing one spending use against another are the budget choices of topic 4.1.
