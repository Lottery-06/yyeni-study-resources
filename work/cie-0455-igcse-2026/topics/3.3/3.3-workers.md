## Choosing an occupation

### What is being chosen

An occupation is a person's regular type of work - the job they train for and do, such as fitting machines or teaching. Choosing one means weighing the wage on offer, the pay for a period of work, against non-wage factors: the skills and qualifications a person already holds, the training a job adds, working conditions and hours, security of employment, distance from home and family, personal interest and aptitude, and the status a job carries. The work on offer sits in the three sectors of topic 3.5 - taking materials from nature, making things, or providing services.

### Why the same offers suit different people

Each person weights the same factors differently. A school-leaver may take a lower-paid post that trains her as a chef, because the training builds future earnings and fits her interest. A parent of three may prefer a day-shift job near home to a distant post paying N$900 more, because security and family count for more in her situation. A worker retrenched once may rank security of employment above each other factor. Because priorities differ, the occupation chosen is the package - pay together with conditions, training and location - that best fits the chooser, not the post where the wage alone is highest.

### Two offers in one week

Tulipamwe, 23, finished a fitting course at a technical centre in Windhoek. In one week she held two offers: N$8 200 a month as a machine fitter at Oshakati Textiles, day shifts, near her family; and N$9 000 a month as a line worker at a fish-processing plant in Walvis Bay, 500 km away, on night shifts. The pay gap is N$9 000 - N$8 200 = N$800 a month. She took the fitter post: the extra pay did not outweigh the night work, the distance and the lost family support, and the fitter post extends a certified skill. In Germany a nurse weighing a hospital post with rotating shifts against a clinic post with steady hours makes the same comparison, and may take the clinic even where it pays less.

### Workers are classified by their industry, not by rank

Learners sometimes label people "primary workers" as though primary described the worker. The primary, secondary and tertiary labels classify industries by what they do: fishing off Walvis Bay takes materials from nature, Oshakati Textiles makes things, a lodge at Etosha provides services. A worker belongs to a sector through the industry that employs them, and "primary" names the sector, not the worker's seniority or importance. The test: ask what the industry does with materials - takes them from nature, turns them into products, or supplies services - and that names the sector the worker sits in.

## How the wage is determined

### The wage as the price of work

A wage is the pay a worker receives for a period of work, quoted in N$ an hour or N$ a month. It is determined in the market for that work: employers demand workers to produce goods and services, workers supply their time and skill, and the wage settles where the number of workers sought matches the number offering to work. Because each occupation and each town has its own pool of workers, the balance - and so the wage - differs from job to job and from place to place.

### Shortage bids up, surplus presses down

Where a new mine near Karibib seeks more trained workers than the town holds at the going wage, employers are likely to bid the wage up to attract and keep staff, because each vacancy left unfilled costs them output. Where a firm closes a production line, more workers offer themselves than employers seek, and pay stalls or falls as job-seekers outnumber posts. A rise in demand for a firm's product works the same way: the firm seeks more workers, and the competition among employers for a limited number of trained people pushes the wage up.

### A packing line in Gobabis

Marula Foods in Gobabis packed fruit with 40 workers at N$5 200 a month. A new pulp contract called for 55 packers; the firm raised the wage to N$6 100, and 15 workers arrived from nearby farms within the month. The extra posts number 55 - 40 = 15 workers, and at the new wage the number sought matched the number offering. In the Netherlands the same balance sets the wage for trained flower sorters at the Aalsmeer auctions: in the weeks before holidays each auction house needs its orders packed at once, and the houses bid up the sorting wage for as long as the rush lasts.

## Why earnings differ

### The reasons behind a pay gap

Earnings - the pay a worker receives over a period, including overtime and bonuses - differ because occupations differ. Skill and qualifications: a chartered accountant's work demands years of study. Scarcity: where few people hold what employers need, pay rises. Experience and responsibility: a mine shift supervisor answers for others and earns more than a new hire. Risk and conditions: a long-haul driver earns more than a pump attendant with a similar training period, because the job takes the driver from home for days at a stretch and employers must pay enough to keep people in it. Productivity and the balance of demand and supply in the occupation set the rest, and where a small number of large employers fix pay scales, their scales hold earnings down.

### Reading the gaps

A 2026 survey of average monthly earnings in Namibia found a petrol-station attendant on N$4 800, a school teacher with ten years in the job on N$14 200, and a chartered accountant on N$38 000. The accountant earns N$38 000 - N$4 800 = N$33 200 more than the attendant. Qualifying takes years of study and fees, few complete it, and employers compete for those who do. The teacher also holds a degree, yet many qualified teachers apply for posts against pay scales set by a small number of large employers, so the scarcity premium is missing. In Brazil, certified welders in the Rio Grande shipyards earn well above general welders for the same reason: certification is scarce, and the yards bid for it.

### The forces behind a difference, drawn in words

Picture the market for certified water engineers. The vertical axis measures the monthly wage in N$; the horizontal axis measures the number of certified engineers. At a low wage few engineers offer their services and employers seek many, so the number sought exceeds the number offered; at a high wage the reverse holds. The two lines cross at the wage where the numbers match. When new water-treatment projects raise the demand for engineers, the demand line moves outward: at the old wage employers now seek more engineers than the region holds, so the wage is bid up until the numbers match again at a higher wage, with more engineers at work. Over the years that follow, the higher wage draws students into training, the supply line moves outward too, and the premium narrows - provided employers' demand has stopped growing faster than the new supply.

## Division of labour and specialisation

### One task each

The division of labour, also called specialisation, splits production into separate narrow tasks so that each worker concentrates on one task instead of making the whole product. Namib Signs in Walvis Bay once built each signboard from design to fitting; after the split, one worker designs, one cuts metal, one paints, one fits - and the boards move from task to task along the workshop.

### Why output rises

Practice at one narrow task builds speed and skill; no time is lost moving between tools and stages; training a new worker for one task takes less time than teaching the whole craft; and each worker settles into the task that suits them. An assembly plant in Nagoya, Japan, grew on this pattern: a worker who spends the year tightening one set of bolts does it faster and with fewer faults than a craft worker who builds whole vehicles.

### What the worker gives up

Repeating one narrow task may bring boredom and low job satisfaction, and it ties the worker's livelihood to that single task: where the product changes or demand for it falls, the specialised worker's risk of losing work grows, because moving means learning a new task from the start. Drawbacks at the level of the whole economy - depending on a narrow range of production - belong to the economy, not to the person at the bench, and answers must keep the two apart.

### Whose benefit, whose drawback?

Asked how the division of labour benefits workers, some learners write that the firm's training costs fall - a benefit to the firm, not to the worker doing the task. Asked for a drawback to workers, some write about a country depending on a single export - a drawback to the economy. The test is to ask whose position the answer changes: the person at the bench, the business employing them, or the whole economy. Naming the party the question asks about is what earns the marks.

### Connections
