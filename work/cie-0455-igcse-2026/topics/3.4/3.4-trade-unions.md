## What a trade union is

### The definition

A trade union is an organisation of workers, joined and funded by them, that aims to protect and improve its members' pay and working conditions. Membership is normally voluntary: a worker who joins pays a subscription, and the money pays the union's organisers and offices. The point of joining is strength in numbers. One cleaner asking alone for a higher rate carries little weight, whereas a union speaking for all the cleaners at once puts the case for all of them together. Unions usually organise by trade or by workplace, so their members share an employer or a type of work.

### Two unions, two countries

At Oshakati Textiles, the machinists belong to the Textile Workers' Union, which negotiates their hourly rate with the firm each year. In Japan, workers at an electronics assembly plant in Nagoya belong to a plant union of the same kind: members pay a subscription, elect their organisers, and the union bargains with the plant's management over pay and holidays. Different economies, same shape - organisations of workers, funded by subscriptions, acting for members' pay and conditions.

### Not everything with trade in it is a union

Do not let the word trade mislead you. A trade union has no link to trade between nations, the buying and selling of goods across borders taught elsewhere in the syllabus. The test is membership and purpose: a trade union's members are workers, and it bargains with employers over pay and conditions; a body that promotes a country's exports has firms or the government behind it and does not bargain for anyone's wages.

## The role of trade unions in the economy

### What unions do

A union's first role is collective bargaining: it negotiates with the employer over the pay and working conditions of all its members at once. Around that core sit the other roles: protecting members' rights, representing them in disputes such as an unfair dismissal or a disagreement over shift patterns, and pressing for safer workplaces and training that raises members' skills. A settlement at a large workplace also tends to shape pay in the same trade elsewhere, because employers nearby compete for the same workers and are likely to match the rate. That is why unions matter to the economy and not merely to one firm: through many local bargains they influence wages across the labour market.

### One bargain at a cannery

At Kuiseb Fish Processors, a cannery in Walvis Bay, 240 of the 300 workers belong to the Marine Workers' Union. In 2026 the union negotiated an hourly rise from N$12.50 to N$14.00, and the cannery paid the higher rate to all 300. One member alone is a single worker among three hundred; the union, speaking for 240 of them, is a party the cannery has to take seriously, because replacing a workforce of that size would halt the plant. It extended the rise to non-members too, since two pay rates in one cannery breed resentment. In Germany, the union at a Hamburg bus company bargains the same way: one agreement covers the drivers' pay and shift rosters.

### Three boundaries worth learning

Three boundaries keep this topic clean. A trade union is not trade between nations: goods crossing borders belong to international trade, while the union's business is pay and conditions. A trade union is not a trade organisation: a body promoting a country's exports serves firms and the government, not workers' wages. And a question about unions' role in the economy asks what they do across the labour market, while a question about firms asks what happens inside one employer. The test: who acts, and on whose pay.

## Advantages and disadvantages of union activity

### What unions can achieve

For workers the advantage is direct: a negotiated rise lifts pay, and negotiated rules make the workplace safer and treatment more predictable. For the firm there can be an offsetting gain: workers who feel fairly dealt with tend to stay, so the firm recruits and trains fewer replacements, and a settled relationship with the union loses fewer days to disputes. For the economy, unions bargaining for low-paid workers tend to narrow the gap between low and higher pay, and the income they win is spent in local shops - traders near the Oshakati textile plant see it each pay day.

### Where the costs land

The costs are just as real. A wage rise won in bargaining raises the firm's pay bill hour by hour: at Marula Meat Processors, an abattoir in Gobabis, a deal lifting the rate from N$10.00 to N$11.00 an hour added N$1.00 an hour for as long as it employs its 180 union members. A firm facing dearer labour may raise prices, and where its buyers can switch to cheaper imports it may instead let its workforce shrink by not replacing workers who leave. Industrial action is the sharpest cost: a three-day stoppage at the abattoir lost N$96 000 of output, and the striking workers lost three days' pay. Dock unions at the port of Rotterdam in the Netherlands know the same arithmetic.

### What the balance depends on

Whether union activity helps or harms depends on three conditions. Whether the pay rise matches output per worker: where it does, the firm can pay the rise out of extra production; where it does not, costs rise with no extra income to cover them. Whether the economy is creating jobs: in a growing economy a firm can carry higher pay and keep hiring; in a weak one it sheds them. And whether the union and the employer bargain or fight: a settlement reached at a table costs both sides less than one reached on a picket line.
