## Where demand meets supply

### Equilibrium, defined

Market equilibrium is the position of a market in which the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied. The price at which the two match is the equilibrium price, and the quantity bought and sold there is the equilibrium quantity. At the Opuwo market, Kaoko Juice finds that at N$10 a bottle buyers are willing and able to take 100 bottles a week and sellers are willing and able to offer 100: the market clears - no bottles left over, no buyer walking away - so N$10 is the equilibrium price and 100 bottles the equilibrium quantity.

### Finding the equilibrium from the schedules

Work price by price. Kaoko Juice's demand schedule: at N$8 a bottle, 120 bottles a week are demanded; at N$10, 100; at N$12, 80. Its supply schedule: at N$8, 80 are offered; at N$10, 100; at N$12, 120. At N$8 buyers want 40 more than sellers offer, and at N$12 sellers offer 40 more than buyers take, so the match sits at N$10, where both sides stand at 100 bottles. On a diagram, price goes on the vertical axis and bottles a week on the horizontal; the demand curve slopes down, the supply curve slopes up, and their crossing point marks the equilibrium: N$10 and 100 bottles.

### A shift carries the market to a new equilibrium

A good marula season raises the supply of juice: at each price, sellers now offer 40 more bottles. At the old price of N$10 the market no longer clears, because sellers offer 140 bottles while buyers still take 100, a surplus of 40 bottles, since 140 - 100 = 40 bottles. Because the stock sits unsold, sellers cut the price; as it slides, the quantity demanded rises along the demand curve while the quantity offered eases along the new supply curve, and the market settles where the two match again: N$8 and 120 bottles. A rise in supply has carried the equilibrium to a lower price and a larger quantity - the same steps a bicycle dealer in Vietnam runs through when clearing a big delivery with a price cut.

## When the market does not clear

### Disequilibrium, surplus and shortage

Market disequilibrium is a position of a market in which the quantity demanded does not equal the quantity supplied at the prevailing price: the price sits above or below its equilibrium level. Above it, sellers offer more than buyers take - a surplus. Below it, buyers want more than sellers offer - a shortage. In Swakopmund, jam clears at N$30 a jar; held at N$40, jars demanded fall to 120 while jars offered rise to 180 - a surplus of 60 jars, since 180 - 120 = 60 jars.

### Measuring the gap at a price

The gap belongs to its price. The Swakopmund jam schedule: at N$20 a jar, 190 jars are demanded and 90 offered - a shortage of 100 jars, since 190 - 90 = 100 jars; at N$30, 150 and 150, no gap; at N$40, the surplus again. The woven-basket stall nearby shows the same working: with the equilibrium at N$45, a trader holding N$60 faces 60 baskets demanded and 90 offered, a surplus of 30 baskets, since 90 - 60 = 30 baskets. A noodle bar in Ho Chi Minh City pricing its bowls low shows the reverse: a queue, a shortage at work.

### How the pressure closes the gap

Take the shortage at N$20. Because buyers miss out, some of them are likely to offer more, and the price is bid up; as it rises, the quantity demanded eases along the demand curve while the quantity supplied stretches along the supply curve, and the gap narrows until the market is back at N$30 and 150 jars. A surplus works in reverse: unsold jars earn nothing, so the trader is likely to cut the price until the shelf empties. The pressure runs one way - a surplus presses the price down, a shortage presses it up - and the market tends back towards equilibrium.

### The test that tells them apart

The test is two readings: where the price sits against the equilibrium level, and which quantity exceeds which. Price above equilibrium, quantity supplied the larger - a surplus. Price below equilibrium, quantity demanded the larger - a shortage. The figures at the stated price settle it.
