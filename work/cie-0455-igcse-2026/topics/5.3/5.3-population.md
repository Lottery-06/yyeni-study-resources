## The factors that affect population growth

### What population growth is

Population growth is the change in the number of people living in a country over a period. Three factors drive it. The birth rate is the number of live births per 1000 people in a country in a year, so a birth rate of 24 per 1000 in a district around Oshakati means 24 babies born for every 1000 residents in the year. The death rate is the number of deaths per 1000 people in a year. Net migration is the balance between immigration, people moving into a country to live, and emigration, people moving out to live elsewhere; the two are usually reported together as one net figure per 1000 people per year.

### Natural increase and total growth in figures

Natural increase is the birth rate minus the death rate. A country with a birth rate of 24 per 1000 people per year and a death rate of 8 per 1000 has a natural increase of 16 per 1000: 24 - 8 = 16, so its population is growing before migration is counted. Overall growth then adds net migration: where net migration is inward at 3 per 1000 people per year, total growth is 19 per 1000, because 16 + 3 = 19. Japan's figures run the other way: a birth rate of 7 per 1000 against a death rate of 12 per 1000 means deaths outnumber births by 5 per 1000, so unless immigration fills the gap the population shrinks.

### Migration changes the count too

Migration moves people across borders and so changes a country's population without a single birth or death. Workers have moved to Walvis Bay for port and fishing jobs, adding to the town's count, while Namibians have left to study in the Netherlands or to work in Germany, subtracting from the count at home. Because migration responds to jobs, wages and living conditions, it can change quickly: a new mine or a drought can shift the net figure within a few years, faster than birth and death rates move.

## Why population growth rates differ between countries

### Why birth and death rates differ

Birth rates tend to be higher in low-income countries, where children contribute to household income early and schooling for girls and family planning are harder to reach. In high-income countries such as Germany or Japan, children cost more to raise and educate, and families tend to be small. Death rates follow income and health care: where clinics, clean water and secure food are scarce, more people die young and life expectancy, the average years a newborn would live if the year's pattern of deaths continued, is shorter. As incomes rise the death rate falls - often while births are still many, which is why low-income populations grow quickly.

### Net migration between countries

People move towards work and away from hardship, so the migration side of growth differs widely. Germany draws in workers because its wages and openings are high, while conflict or drought pushes people out of their home countries. Within southern Africa, movement follows mines, ports and harvests: a worker leaving Oshakati for Windhoek shifts the count within the country, whereas leaving for Germany reduces the national count.

### Birth rate or infant mortality rate?

Answers on this objective sometimes swap two measures that sound alike. The birth rate counts live births per 1000 people in a year. The infant mortality rate counts deaths of babies under one per 1000 live births in a year. The boundary: one measures new life against the whole population, the other early deaths against the babies born. The test: ask what is counted and out of what group. A country can have a high birth rate and a falling infant mortality rate together, because better care keeps babies alive without changing how many are born.

### Interest rates and tax rates do not answer it

Some answers drift into policy terms: interest rates, the price a borrower pays a lender for borrowed money, or tax rates, the compulsory share of income or spending taken by the government. The boundary: interest is a price agreed with a lender, whereas a tax is a payment required by law. The test: ask who receives the payment and whether it is compulsory. Neither drives population growth, which turns on the birth rate, the death rate and net migration, so an answer built on borrowing costs has left the question.

## The effects of changes in population size and structure

### Optimum population

The optimum population is the size of population that, with a country's existing resources and technology, gives the highest output per person. Below it, too few workers make full use of the land and equipment available, so output per person sits lower than it could. Above it, extra people stretch resources and capital thin, so output per person falls. The optimum is not fixed: better technology, more equipment or new resources move it, so a country can grow into a population that once seemed too large.

### When the population grows

A rising population changes what a country needs. More children mean more school places: a fast-growing district may need new classrooms at Oshakati while the taxpayers who fund them rise more slowly. In time the extra children reach working age, and where jobs exist the labour supply grows, which can raise the economy's output. Where jobs, water or land do not keep pace, the arrivals count as dependants rather than earners, and output per person tends to fall. Brazil's cities grew this quickly: industry expanded, but housing and services lagged behind the arrivals.

### When the population ages and shifts

A population can change in structure rather than size. Where the birth rate falls and life expectancy lengthens, the share of older people rises, as in Japan: the workforce shrinks relative to the retired, pension and health costs borne by the government and by workers climb, and firms face labour shortages. Where the share under 15 is large, schooling is paid for first and the taxes those children pay come much later. Gender distribution matters too: where men migrate alone for mining work, some regions hold more women of working age, shifting who supports households.

### Reading a population pyramid

A population pyramid sets the number or share of males and females at each age side by side, rising up the age groups. A wide base relative to the top indicates high birth rates and a young population. A narrow base with a broad upper section indicates low birth rates, longer lives and an ageing population. Reading it answers two questions: how fast the next generation of workers replaces the current one, and how heavy the dependent groups at the top and bottom are relative to the working ages between them.
